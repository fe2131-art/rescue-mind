\title{Verify, Look Again, Remember:\\Fail-Closed Vision-Language Checks and Safety-Monotone Memory\\for Relief Delivery Robots}
\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Anonymous Authors}\IEEEauthorblockA{Code, data and every trial log: \url{https://github.com/fe2131-art/rescue-mind}}}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
In disaster relief, the inventory a delivery robot is told to trust often disagrees with what is actually at a supply
point: items are swapped, shelves are empty, or the site has become dangerous. We present \sys, a mobile robot that
checks the item and the site with a vision-language model (VLM) on a local GPU before it carries anything to a shelter.
The check is built to fail closed: decisions come from the next-token probabilities of answer letters in closed-set
questions, never from free text, and motion stays behind a velocity guard that the VLM cannot command.
Running the full stack exposed two gaps, and we close each with a simple, verifiable rule.
First, fires partly hidden behind the supply escape a single frontal view, so the robot re-views the station from
0.4\,m to either side before it leaves. In a paired, pre-registered campaign this reduced deliveries despite a
perceivable fire from \GWrongB{} to \GWrongC{} of \GPairs{} (exact McNemar $p=\GMcNemarP$), with no false stops.
Second, a robot that resupplies the same shelter can remember what it saw, but stale memory is dangerous. We let memory
only reorder where the robot looks and add caution, never remove a check, which bounds the per-mission probability of an
unsafe delivery by the number of stations checked times the per-check false-approval rate, however stale the memory is.
In \HPTwoN{} paired missions per arm in worlds that change between missions, unsafe deliveries were \HUnsafeMtwo{} with
our memory, \HUnsafeMzero{} without memory and \HUnsafeMone{} with a naive memory that skips checks; where supplies
stayed relocated, the median mission time fell from \HTimeMzero\,s to \HTimeMtwo\,s.
Every evaluation was pre-registered and every closed-loop trial audited against simulator ground truth:
\ECorrect{}/\EN{} expected outcomes in randomised worlds, \RPoolFA{} false approvals under \RNcond{} camera
degradations, against \ZSigTOneFA{} for a zero-shot SigLIP baseline on held-out images. On real photographs the hazard
check found \PRecall{} fire or smoke images but also flagged \PFalseAlarm{} fire-free ones, so a site-specific
threshold is needed before deployment. We release code, worlds, ledgers and every amendment.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Relief logistics is a ``last-mile'' problem: goods move from local depots to the people who need them~\cite{balcik2008lastmile}, under conditions in which records are stale and sites change by the hour~\cite{murphy2014disaster}. A robot that delivers whatever its inventory says is at a station may hand a
shelter a first-aid kit when the shelter asked for water, report a delivery from an empty shelf, or, because an inventory
says nothing about hazards, drive into a fire. Vision-language models (VLMs) can often describe such situations
without task-specific training and are used to explain or detect robot failures~\cite{liu2023reflect,duan2025aha,agia2025sentinel}.
Their outputs, however, are biased by option order and answer letters~\cite{zheng2024mcselectors,pezeshkpour2024order},
they hallucinate objects that are not there~\cite{li2023pope}, and free-form generation can be unparseable.
We saw all three on our own images (Supp.~Sec.~S8).

Relief robots also return to the same shelter again and again, and a robot that remembers where the water
really was, or where it saw smoke, could be faster. Yet acting on stale memory can hurt: in a FrozenLake-style grid world with six VLMs, an agent that trusted unaudited memory failed more
often than one with no memory~\cite{sun2026memorylies}, and a disaster site changes between missions. So, \emph{how can
a robot benefit from memory without letting memory weaken its checks?}

We therefore ask a narrow, safety-motivated question: \emph{can an off-the-shelf VLM running on the robot
serve as a pre-action gate that rarely approves a wrong delivery, while still approving correct ones, and can
we show this with evidence a skeptical reader can audit?} Our answers are a system design and an evaluation
methodology:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A fail-closed checkpoint} (Sec.~\ref{sec:checkpoint}). Two permuted multiple-choice questions and a Yes/No hazard
question are scored by answer-letter probabilities; the robot approves only if they agree
and clear a confidence threshold, as in a selective classifier~\cite{geifman2017selective}. A wrong item triggers a one-time search of the other station, a hazard never does.
\item \textbf{Active look-around} (Sec.~\ref{sec:fix}). A fire hidden behind the supply is often plainly visible from the side,
so after an approval the robot re-checks from two side poses, and any hazard there revokes the approval.
\item \textbf{Safety-monotone mission memory} (Sec.~\ref{sec:memory}). Memory proposes, perception disposes: it may reorder the stations the robot
visits and tighten its hazard threshold, but never skip a check. We state the invariant, enforce it in code and in the audit, and test it in a pre-registered
multi-mission campaign against a naive memory.
\item \textbf{The original two-model design on one GPU} (Sec.~\ref{sec:jev}). We fill the second checker's role with Clef-Flash,
which Cloudflare post-trained from Qwen3.5-9B~\cite{cloudflare2026clefcard}, require both to approve, and run the pair on the simulated robot.
\item \textbf{Decision and motion separated, in the spirit of Simplex}~\cite{seto1998simplex,sha2001simplicity}: the mission node only
\emph{requests} velocities, and an independent guard is the sole publisher of wheel commands.
\item \textbf{Auditable, pre-registered evaluation} (Sec.~\ref{sec:method}). Prompts and thresholds were chosen
on development images only; a 600-image held-out test set and the closed-loop
campaigns D--J and V were registered before running~\cite{nosek2018preregistration,hofman2023preregistration}.
Each closed-loop trial is checked against Gazebo ground truth, a ROS bag and a recomputation of the decision,
and every attempt is kept. The studies in Table~\ref{tab:studies} include a camera-degradation
study.
\item \textbf{Lessons from running the real stack} (Supp.~Sec.~S8): \NFindings{} failure causes that unit tests and
mocks missed, such as a wheel-axis frame error that let odometry-based audits pass a robot that never arrived.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Terms.} \emph{v3.4} is the first registered checkpoint (hazard threshold $\tauh=0.5$); \emph{v3.5} lowers it to $0.05$;
\emph{v3.6} additionally uses a hazard wording that names small, partly hidden fire and smoke and asks it on three enlarged
crops as well as on the full frame (Sec.~\ref{sec:fix}). A fire is \emph{visible} at a view if a rendered fire-only mask has at
least 614 pixels there, and \emph{perceivable} in a world if this holds in at least one of three views (front checkpoint,
left, right). A \emph{campaign} is a closed-loop study.
\emph{Expected behaviour} is the action the generator's ground truth requires: deliver, deliver after searching
the other station, or refuse because of a wrong item or a hazard.

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{Failure detection and verification.}
REFLECT~\cite{liu2023reflect}, AHA~\cite{duan2025aha} and success detectors~\cite{du2023successdetectors} explain or detect failures after the fact;
Sentinel~\cite{agia2025sentinel} and FAIL-Detect~\cite{xu2025faildetect} monitor learned policies at run time, Code-as-Monitor~\cite{zhou2025codeasmonitor} checks constraints with VLM-generated programs, and an LLM anomaly monitor triggers a safe fallback~\cite{sinha2024realtime} (Supp.~Sec.~S3).
The closest work we know is VAP-TAMP~\cite{oloo2026vaptamp}: before and after each action it asks a VLM paraphrased yes/no questions, takes a majority vote, and moves the camera if the view is insufficient; the vote updates the state used for replanning. To our knowledge, ours differs in closed-set next-token scores, a fail-closed conjunction (a failed check can only refuse or revoke), an independent velocity guard, memory limited to reordering and caution, and pre-registered closed-loop audits against ground truth.
KnowNo~\cite{ren2023knowno} scores multiple-choice answers by next-token probability and asks a human for help with a conformal guarantee~\cite{angelopoulos2021conformal}; bounding a miss rate with high probability is risk control~\cite{angelopoulos2024conformalrisk,angelopoulos2025ltt};
SayCan~\cite{ichter2023saycan} likewise scores a fixed candidate set. We share the scoring but refuse or search under uncertainty, and add permuted-option agreement against letter bias~\cite{zheng2024mcselectors,pezeshkpour2024order,robinson2023mcqa}. Language models are fairly calibrated on formatted multiple-choice questions~\cite{kadavath2022know}, but confidence still needs validation~\cite{guo2017calibration,zhao2021calibrate}, and embodied VLMs abstain poorly on their own~\cite{wu2025abstaineqa}.

\textbf{Safety architectures.} Simplex~\cite{seto1998simplex,sha2001simplicity}, shields~\cite{alshiekh2018shielding}
and control barrier functions~\cite{ames2019cbf} keep a simple verified layer between a complex decision maker and
the actuators (see~\cite{hsu2024safetyfilter}). LLM-controlled robots can be manipulated into harmful actions~\cite{robey2025jailbreaking}; RoboGuard~\cite{ravichandran2026guardrails} checks plans first. Unlike vision-language-action models~\cite{zitkovich2023rt2,kim2025openvla}, our VLM never commands motion.

\textbf{Memory and active perception.}
Semantic memory~\cite{anwar2025remembr,wang2025karma} and 3D scene graphs~\cite{hughes2022hydra,gu2024conceptgraphs} \emph{answer} or \emph{plan}~\cite{rana2023sayplan}; mapping has long dealt with change~\cite{biber2005dynamic,krajnik2017fremen}. In a grid-world study, unaudited stale memory was worse than none~\cite{sun2026memorylies};
GLOVE~\cite{yin2026glove} and VLM-DEWM~\cite{tang2026vlmdewm} check memory against fresh observations. We instead use memory to decide \emph{where and how carefully to verify}, never to reduce verification.
Object search with belief priors~\cite{aydemir2013active,wandzel2019mos,zeng2020slim,zhou2023esc} and semi-static maps~\cite{mason2012objectworld,rosen2016lifelong} also use past observations to order search; to our knowledge they trust detections and do not bound the risk of stale memory at a pre-action gate.
Moving the sensor to resolve ambiguity is classic~\cite{bajcsy1988active,bajcsy2018revisiting}, and agents explore until a VLM is confident~\cite{ren2024exploreuntil}; ours is deliberately simple: two fixed side views, used only to \emph{revoke} an approval.

\textbf{Robustness, redundancy and evaluation.} Classifiers, detectors and VLMs degrade under common
corruptions~\cite{hendrycks2019benchmarking,michaelis2019benchmarking,chen2023benchmarking,schiappa2022robustness}; we measure one part of the sim-to-real gap~\cite{hofer2021sim2real,li2024simpler}, sensor degradation, and care about the \emph{direction} of errors. Agreement between independent versions is a classic
fault-tolerance technique~\cite{avizienis1985nversion} limited by correlated failures~\cite{knight1986experimental}. We follow recommendations to report conditions, metrics, uncertainty
and failure modes~\cite{kressgazit2024empirical}, with Wilson intervals~\cite{wilson1927probable}, exact McNemar tests~\cite{mcnemar1947note} and domain randomisation~\cite{tobin2017domain} for the held-out \emph{test} set.

\section{System}\label{sec:system}
\textbf{Platform.} A differential-drive robot with a 2D lidar and a mast camera (640$\times$480, 1.05\,rad
horizontal field of view) runs in Gazebo Harmonic with ROS~2 Jazzy~\cite{macenski2022ros2,koenig2004gazebo}
(Fig.~\ref{fig:system}). The arena has two supply stations (water, medicine) and a shelter.
The VLM runs in FP16 on a single Tesla V100 (32\,GB). We use Qwen3.5-9B~\cite{qwen2026qwen35}
(Apache-2.0), selected over Qwen2.5-VL-3B~\cite{bai2025qwen25vl}, Qwen3-VL-8B~\cite{bai2025qwen3vl} and Qwen3.5-4B on
development data (Sec.~\ref{sec:method}).

\textbf{Mission.} A request (Japanese or English) is parsed into a supply and a
destination by two closed-set VLM questions (supply: water / medicine / both / other; destination:
shelter~B / another place / unstated). The robot accepts the parse only if the most likely item is water or medicine with $p\ge 0.6$,
the most likely destination is not ``another place'' (shelter~B is the only shelter), and an explicit rule finds no other
shelter name; otherwise it refuses before moving. It then drives to the station, aims at its centre, waits until stationary, and captures fresh frames whose time and image quality are checked.

\subsection{Fail-closed checkpoint}\label{sec:checkpoint}
For an image $x$ and requested supply $s^\ast$, the VLM is asked two differently worded questions about what is on
the platform, with permuted options:
\begin{itemize}
\item $q_1$: A drinking water / B first-aid kit / C nothing or another object;
\item $q_2$: A neither (empty or a different object) / B first-aid kit / C drinking water.
\end{itemize}
It is also asked (once per image) the hazard question $q_h$: ``Look at the whole image, including behind and around the objects.
Do you see any fire, flames, or smoke? Yes/No''. 
For each question we compute a softmax over the log-sum-exp of the next-token logits of each answer symbol's
tokenizer variants. This gives $p_k(s)$ for $k\in\{1,2\}$ and $p_h$. The robot approves iff
\begin{equation}
\bigwedge_{k=1,2}\Big[\arg\max_s p_k(s)=s^\ast \;\wedge\; p_k(s^\ast)\ge\taus\Big]\;\wedge\;p_h<\tauh,
\label{eq:rule}\end{equation}
with $\taus=0.5$ (fixed on development data and unchanged since the first registration) and $\tauh=0.05$.
In v3.6 it is also asked on three enlarged crops, and $p_h$ is the maximum over the full frame and the crops.
We call $p_k$ and $p_h$ probabilities for brevity: they are the next-token mass of the answer letters, renormalised over the
allowed answers, and are scores to be thresholded, not calibrated probabilities (on real photographs their scale shifts; Sec.~\ref{sec:results}).
The hazard threshold was 0.5 up to campaign D and was lowered once (v3.5) after a pilot missed a mostly occluded fire
(Supp.~Sec.~S8). The asymmetry is deliberate: a missed fire can hurt people, while a false alarm only stops the robot.
Every failure leads to refusal: inference errors, non-finite logits (FP16 overflow guard), unknown labels, images
with different hashes, or a model not on the allow-list.
A refusal is followed by a 2.5\,s zero-velocity hold that the audit checks.

\textbf{Recovery and hazard policy.} After a wrong or missing item the robot checks the
other station once with the same rule; after a hazard refusal it never searches, but stops and reports.
After every mission a one-page operator report is generated from the robot's own log: every check image with its
decision and probabilities, the memory's proposals and changes, and a concrete next action (e.g.\ ``ask the fire
service to check station~M'', ``update the inventory: the medicine is at station~W'').

\textbf{Velocity guard.} The guard publishes zero unless camera, lidar and odometry are fresh and the
front sector is clear. A stale \emph{motion} request latches the guard and ends the mission.
A stale \emph{zero} request holds zero without latching (Supp.~Sec.~S8). A graph watcher triggers an
emergency stop if any other node publishes \texttt{/cmd\_vel}.

\subsection{Active look-around}\label{sec:lookaround}
A fire behind the supply is occluded by the very object the robot is checking. A larger model cannot see
pixels that are not in the image, so the robot moves instead of thinking harder, in the spirit of active perception~\cite{bajcsy1988active,bajcsy2018revisiting}. After the front check approves, the robot drives to two poses 0.4\,m to
the left and right of the checkpoint, perpendicular to the line of sight. At each pose it aims at the station centre,
waits until stationary, captures fresh frames and asks the same questions. Only the hazard answer is used there, because
the supply may be out of view from the side. Any side view with a hazard turns the approval into a hazard refusal, with
no search (Fig.~\ref{fig:lookaround}). Formally, with side views $v\in V=\{L,R\}$, the approval becomes
$A = A_{\mathrm{front}} \wedge \bigwedge_{v\in V} [\,p_{h}^{(v)} < \tauh\,]$. The audit recomputes every side decision. In campaign G it added a median of \GLookCost\,s to a delivery without a hazard (from \GWallPlainB\,s to \GWallPlainC\,s
over the same fire-free worlds, Sec.~\ref{sec:fix}).

\subsection{A second checker}\label{sec:jev}
The original design of this robot asked two models (a second checker, called Jev in that design, and Qwen) and moved only when both agreed, following the
idea of diverse redundancy~\cite{avizienis1985nversion}, although failures are known to be correlated in practice~\cite{knight1986experimental}. The second checker is Clef-Flash, a decision model released by Cloudflare~\cite{cloudflare2026clefcard,chen2026clefblog} and served locally through the original second-checker API.
According to its model card it is post-trained from Qwen3.5-9B, so the two models share lineage. Their diversity lies in
post-training, in model size (our on-robot pair uses Qwen3.5-4B) and in the question asked, and the correlated-failure
caveat applies in full. The two-model rule approves only if Eq.~\eqref{eq:rule} holds for Qwen \emph{and}
Clef-Flash agrees (thresholds: Supp.~Sec.~S1). A hazard seen by either model stops the robot, and a wrong supply seen by either
allows the one-time search. Whether the two models fit one GPU is
tested in Sec.~\ref{sec:res-jev}. The second model is a veto whose errors overlap with Qwen's. Qwen misses \JevOvQ{} of \JevOvN{} development fires, and Clef-Flash also misses \JevOvBoth{} of these (\JevOvCond, Wilson \JevOvCondCI; Supp.~Table~S9).

\subsection{Safety-monotone mission memory}\label{sec:memory}
After every mission the robot appends what \emph{it} observed to a memory file; ground truth is never written.
For each checked station the record holds the supply both passes named with $p\ge0.5$ (or \emph{uncertain}), whether a hazard was seen,
and from which views (front, left, right).
Before the next mission, memory may change exactly two things.
(i)~\emph{Route proposal:} the robot may visit first the station where it last saw the requested supply, when the supply's own station last
showed something else; that station is still checked, and the one-time search still applies.
(ii)~\emph{Caution:} at a station where a hazard was seen during the last three missions, every view refuses at
$p_h\ge\tau_h^{\mathrm{mem}}=0.01$ instead of $\tauh=0.05$, and the side that revealed the hazard is looked at first.

Let $\theta=(\taus,\tauh,V)$ be the parameters of the check at a station, and let $A_\theta(o)\in\{0,1\}$ be its approval
for observations $o$ (all frames and model outputs at that station). Memory maps the history $\mathcal{H}$ to
$\theta'=\mu(\mathcal{H})$ and to a visiting order $\pi(\mathcal{H})$. We call a memory policy \emph{verification-preserving}
if, at every station it checks, $\taus'\ge\taus$, $\tauh'\le\tauh$ and $V'\supseteq V$. It must also approve a delivery
only through $A_{\theta'}$ at the delivering station.

\textbf{Observation 1 (per-check monotonicity).} For a verification-preserving policy, $A_{\theta'}(o)\le A_\theta(o)$
for every $o$. $A_\theta$ is a conjunction of a supply condition, which is non-increasing in $\taus$, and of hazard
conditions $[p_h^{(v)}<\tauh]$, which are non-increasing as $\tauh$ decreases. Adding views adds conjuncts, and the order of
the views does not change a conjunction. It is elementary by design, so code and audit can enforce it.

The interesting question is what this implies for whole missions, because memory also changes \emph{which} stations are
checked. Let $\varepsilon$ bound the probability that the memory-free check $A_\theta$ approves a must-refuse view of a
station (a wrong or missing supply, or a perceivable fire). The bound must hold for every station view the robot can
encounter.

\textbf{Corollary 1 (per-mission risk).} A verification-preserving policy delivers only after $A_{\theta'}$, and hence
$A_\theta$, approved the delivering station. A mission checks at most $k$ stations ($k=2$ here: the first station and the
one-time search). An unsafe delivery therefore requires a false approval at one of at most $k$ must-refuse checks, and by the
union bound $\Pr[\text{unsafe delivery}]\le k\varepsilon$ for every visiting order $\pi$ and however stale $\mathcal{H}$ is.
The memory-free robot satisfies the same bound, with the same $k$. A naive policy that delivers on $\mathcal{H}$ alone has no such bound: its risk is the
probability that the world changed since it last looked, which the robot cannot observe without looking.

Two caveats keep this honest. First, $\varepsilon$ is a property of the check on the views it meets; a different route meets different views, so the bound
transfers only to the extent that $\varepsilon$ is uniform over stations. We measure the per-check rates (E, G, T1, T2)
and the mission-level outcome (H) rather than assume them. Second, the corollary bounds risk, not time: a stale route proposal costs a detour, which campaign H and the change-rate
model measure.

\begin{algorithm}[t]\caption{One mission: verify, look again, search or stop, remember}\label{alg:mission}
\small\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require request $r$, memory $\mathcal{H}$
\State $s^\ast \gets$ parse$(r)$; \textbf{if} rejected \textbf{then} refuse before moving
\State $(\pi, \theta') \gets \mu(\mathcal{H}, s^\ast)$ \Comment{order of stations; $\tauh'\le\tauh$ at remembered hazards}
\For{station $k$ in $\pi$ (at most two)}
  \State drive to $k$; aim; wait until stationary; capture new frames $o_k$
  \State $d \gets$ \textsc{Check}$(o_k, s^\ast; \theta'_k)$ \Comment{Eq.~\eqref{eq:rule}; and the second checker, if used}
  \If{$d=$ hazard} \State hold zero 2.5\,s; report; \textbf{stop} \EndIf
  \If{$d=$ approve}
    \For{side $v$ in order$(\theta'_k)$} \State move 0.4\,m to side $v$; capture $o_{k,v}$
      \If{$p_h(o_{k,v})\ge\tau'_{h,k}$} \State hold; report; \textbf{stop} \EndIf
    \EndFor
    \State deliver to the shelter; \textbf{break}
  \EndIf
  \If{$d=$ low confidence} \textbf{stop} \EndIf
  \State \Comment{wrong or missing supply: try the next station once}
\EndFor
\State $\mathcal{H} \gets \mathcal{H} \cup$ observations$(o)$ \Comment{what the robot saw, never ground truth}
\end{algorithmic}\end{algorithm}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:mission} summarises one mission.
A \emph{naive} memory is not verification-preserving. It replaces $A_\theta(o)$ by a predicate of $\mathcal{H}$
alone: it skips the check where it last saw the supply, and refuses without visiting where it last saw a hazard. We
implement it only as a baseline. The code enforces the premise: \texttt{apply\_memory\_caution} can only turn approvals into
refusals, and the run audit rejects any memory threshold above $\tauh$.

\section{Evaluation Methodology}\label{sec:method}
\textbf{Development data.} Prompts, scoring, thresholds and model choice were selected on 176 development images, the instruction
parser on 40 utterances, and the later hazard fixes (Sec.~\ref{sec:fix}) also on two 300-image hazard sets, DH and DH2
(composition: Supp.~Sec.~S1).

\textbf{Held-out test sets.} \emph{Instructions:} 60 utterances frozen before the parser was built,
covering paraphrases, negation, lexical traps, other shelters and irrelevant requests.
\emph{Images (T1):} 60 Gazebo worlds $\times$ 2 stations $\times$ 5 viewpoints, with continuous random
lighting, supplies moved by up to $\pm$0.15\,m and freely rotated, random fire positions, and three distractors, two of which
(a blue crate and an orange traffic cone, orange but not fire) never appeared during development (examples: Supp.~Fig.~S1).
Labels come from the generator, never from a model. Whether the fire is visible is measured, not assumed:
each view of a fire world is rendered a second time with only flames and smoke drawn in flat magenta, so occlusion by the supply
or the platform is taken into account. A view is \emph{hidden}
with at most 20 fire pixels and \emph{ambiguous} with 21--613; the \TNamb{} ambiguous image(s) are excluded from the primary endpoints, as registered.
Campaign F conditions on visibility at the
checkpoint, campaign G on perceivability. These labels use no model output and are fixed before any trial.

\textbf{Closed-loop campaigns.} Each trial starts a fresh Gazebo, bridge, guard, VLM server, watcher,
recorder and ROS bag. Campaign D (\DN{} trials, v3.4 with $\tauh=0.5$) used the three controlled scenes plus fire, dim light and a box.
Campaign E (\EN{} trials, v3.5) gave every trial its own randomised world, registered before any E trial was run, in the strata
nominal, swapped, empty, fire at the requested station, and fire at the \emph{other} station, where the
search itself must stop.
A trial counts only if an audit confirms it: the decision is recomputed from the saved model outputs and image hash; the
ground-truth trajectory (Gazebo odometry publisher) shows arrival at the shelter after approval, or no motion toward it after a
refusal; odometry agrees with ground truth; and the ROS bag is finalised. Only stack failures \emph{before} any VLM decision may be re-run once. All attempts stay in the ledger.

\textbf{Metrics and statistics.} The primary safety metric is the false-approval rate: approvals among
cases that must be refused because of a wrong or missing item or a visible fire.
It is paired with the correct-approval rate. We report Wilson 95\% intervals~\cite{wilson1927probable}; images of one world
share lighting, props and fire, so pooled rates also get a bootstrap interval that resamples whole worlds. Paired
comparisons use exact McNemar tests~\cite{mcnemar1947note} and, for times, the exact
Wilcoxon signed-rank test. A \emph{safe delivery} is possible when the requested supply is at one of the stations and no
fire burns there. In study R, \emph{pooled false approvals} sum the false approvals over all degraded images and conditions. A claim of improvement is made only if the pre-registered direction and $p<0.05$ both hold;
otherwise we write ``not significant'' and report the counts.

\textbf{Model selection and registration audit.} Qwen3.5-9B was chosen on development data only (zero false approvals and zero
unsafe intent acceptances first, then explanation and intent accuracy) and frozen before the T1 and E registrations (Supp.~Sec.~S2).
For every study with results, the registration commit precedes the results commit (\PreregOrderOK), except campaign C (Supp.~Sec.~S9, Table~S7).

\subsection{Fixing the remaining weakness}\label{sec:fix}
T1 left one failure mode: fires only partly visible behind the supply. Because T1 was then spent, candidate
fixes were compared on development data only, including a new set of small early-stage fires (DH2), since the first hazard set (DH) contained just four visible fires under 10\,000 pixels. The candidates were enlarged overlapping
crops for the hazard question, a hazard wording that names small, partly hidden or cut-off fire and smoke, and Clef-Flash
(Sec.~\ref{sec:jev}). The wording together with the crops is v3.6.
Supp.~Table~S6 shows the results. The wording did most of the work: missed fires fell from \DevFAvThreeFive{} to
\DevFAB{} of \DevFireN{} when combined with crops, and correct approvals did not change. Clef-Flash refused half of the
correct deliveries when asked the original second-checker questions, which concern the safety of a \emph{passage}; with
task-worded questions it reached 97\%, and agreement with Qwen gave the fewest false approvals, but the two models
need 37\,GB together, which does not fit on one V100.

\emph{Held-out confirmation (T2, pre-registered, 600 new images).} The new draw happened to be easy: v3.5 already made
\TTRefFA{} false approvals (\TTRefCA{} correct approvals), so no configuration could improve on it. The configuration selected on development data (Clef-Flash with task wording plus Qwen3.5-9B v3.6, 37\,GB) made
\TTSelFA{} false approvals with \TTSelCA{} correct approvals, and Qwen3.5-9B v3.6 alone made \TTBFA{} with \TTBCA{}.
Clef-Flash \emph{alone}, given the task-worded questions, made \TTClefFA{} false approvals with
\TTClefCA{} correct approvals in a single forward pass (median 0.6\,s per image, against \TimVlm\,s for the scored
Qwen queries; Supp.~Table~S4).

\emph{Closed loop: small fires (campaign F, pre-registered, paired).} Each of 24 worlds had a small fire just behind
a station or no fire, and was run with v3.5 and with v3.6. In the \FPairs{} worlds where the fire
was visible at the checkpoint in both runs, v3.5 delivered despite the fire \FWrongA{} times and v3.6 \FWrongB{} times (exact
McNemar $p=\FMcNemarP$, not significant; \FDiscA{} discordant worlds favoured v3.6 and \FDiscB{} the other).
Neither system stopped falsely in the fire-free runs (v3.5 \FFalseStopA{}, v3.6 \FFalseStopB{}); with so few runs this says little. Six of v3.6's seven remaining misses were small fires whose hazard probabilities overlap
those of fire-free views (Supp.~Sec.~S7).

\subsection{Camera degradations (study R)}\label{sec:res-r}
Gazebo renders are noise-free and well exposed. To probe this part of the sim-to-real gap~\cite{hofer2021sim2real}, we degraded the checkpoint frames of \RNimg{} held-out T2 images
(\RNworlds{} worlds) in \RNcond{} pre-registered ways. There are six families, in the spirit of ImageNet-C~\cite{hendrycks2019benchmarking}: sensor noise, motion blur, defocus, under- and
over-exposure, and JPEG compression, each at a moderate and a severe level (Fig.~\ref{fig:rex}). A frame that the robot's own acquisition gate
rejects (black-out, white-out or flat frame) never reaches the VLM and counts as a refusal.
Supp.~Table~S5 gives every condition. Pooled over all conditions, the check made \RPoolFA{} false approvals, so the registered fail-closed claim is \RPoolSupported. Pooling repeats the same \RNdeny{} must-refuse images, so the image-level bound is per condition: at most \RCondFAMax{} false approvals, Wilson upper bound \RCondFAUpper. \RUnsafeConds{} conditions increased false
approvals significantly. Correct approvals went from \RCleanCA{} on clean frames to \RWorstCA{} in the
worst condition (\RWorstCond). The gate refused \RGateRej{} frames in total, and \RFireMissMax{} of \RNfire{} visible fires were missed in any
condition. Under shift, refusal rather than unsafe approval is the intended failure direction of a selective classifier~\cite{geifman2017selective}.

\textbf{Real photographs (study P).} We also asked the frozen v3.6 hazard question about 400
real photographs from the test split of D-Fire~\cite{venancio2022dfire}, a CC0 fire and smoke dataset
(100 each with no fire, smoke only, fire only, and both). It flagged \PRecall{} of the photographs with fire or smoke (95\% CI
\PRecallCI) and \PFalseAlarm{} of the fire-free ones (\PFalseAlarmCI); the false alarms concentrate on hard negatives such as hazy
hillsides and sunset clouds (Supp.~Sec.~S7). The probabilities still rank the photographs well (AUROC \PAuroc, not registered), so the problem is the threshold, not the question. The question thus errs strongly toward stopping on real images, which is
the safe direction but would cost availability outdoors, so a site-specific threshold calibration is a deployment prerequisite.
Study P2 tested this: it chose wording and $\tau_h$ on 400 separate calibration photographs
(recall $\ge 0.95$ first, then the lower false-alarm rate), with wording \PTwoWording{} and $\tau_h=\PTwoTau$, and applied it to 400 test photographs.
It flagged \PTwoRecall{} of
those with fire or smoke (\PTwoRecallCI) and \PTwoFA{} of the fire-free ones (\PTwoFACI), against \PTwoFrozenRecall{} and
\PTwoFrozenFA{} for the frozen $\tau_h=0.05$. The registered claim (test recall $\ge 0.90$ and false-alarm rate $\le 0.20$) was \PTwoClaim{}.

\subsection{Cross-mission memory (campaign H)}\label{sec:res-h}
Campaign H has 16 sequences of three missions to the same shelter. Lighting and prop placement are re-drawn each mission,
and four scripted stories change the world between missions: the supplies are swapped and stay swapped (\emph{relocation persists}) or
are swapped back (\emph{relocation changes}); a small fire appears and persists; a fire disappears and a new one appears.
The first mission seeds all three arms; missions 2 and 3 run with no memory (M0, v3.6), naive memory (M1) and verified
memory (M2). Table~\ref{tab:h} (per-mission outcomes and times: Supp.~Fig.~S4) summarises the \HPTwoN{} paired missions. On the \HPOneN{} missions whose supplies stayed relocated, the median mission time was \HTimeMtwo\,s for M2
against \HTimeMzero\,s for M0 (exact Wilcoxon
$p=\HPOneP$). M1 made \HUnsafeMone{} unsafe deliveries, against
\HUnsafeMtwo{} for M2 (exact McNemar $p=\HPTwoP$) and \HUnsafeMzero{} for M0. Missions of one sequence share a world and a
memory, so we also test at the sequence level: M1 made at least one unsafe delivery in \HSeqMone{} of \HSeqN{} sequences
and M2 in \HSeqMtwo{} (exact McNemar $p=\HSeqP$). The M1--M2 contrast is large by design, since the naive arm skips checks;
the comparison that bounds our risk is M2 versus M0, \HUnsafeMtwo/32 versus \HUnsafeMzero/32 (Wilson upper bounds
\HUnsafeUppertwo\% and \HUnsafeUpperzero\%), so P3 (M2 $\le$ M0) is met as registered but informative only down to those bounds. False refusals (stops at a station whose fire was already out) were \HFalseRefzero{} for M0, \HFalseRefone{} for M1
and \HFalseReftwo{} for M2. The registered headline claim, verified memory keeping memory's speed-up without its safety cost, is \HHeadline. M1's unsafe deliveries were \HWrongone{} wrong supplies, relocated since it last looked, and \HFireone{} deliveries into a fire that had appeared since; every one of them followed a skipped check. M2 made neither kind of error and no false refusal, and it was faster in \HFasterPairs{} persistent-relocation pairs. A stale proposal has a price: when the supplies had been moved back, M2 first visited the station memory proposed and then searched, and its median mission time was \HChangeMtwo\,s against \HChangeMzero\,s for M0. M0's single unsafe delivery shows what caution is for. In that world the new fire was invisible from the front (0 mask pixels) and small from the side (2\,219 pixels). Without memory, the left view scored $p_h=0.035$, below $\tauh=0.05$, and the robot delivered. Memory had recorded a fire at this station two missions earlier, so M2 checked with $\tau_h^{\mathrm{mem}}=0.01$; its left view scored $p_h=0.049$ and the robot stopped (Supp.~Fig.~S5). This single case does not show that M2 is safer than M0 (1 versus 0 events), only that it is not less safe (P3). It does show the asymmetry at work: memory can make the robot more careful, never less. Caution is local:
a global $\tau_h=0.01$ would refuse correct deliveries on fire-free views (on T2, the conformal threshold
$\tau=\KTauTen$ already raised false alarms to \KFATen, study K), whereas memory applies it only at stations with a recent hazard.
Its cost is not established: caution applied in \HCautN{} of the \HPTwoN{} M2 missions, only \HCautFree{} of them at a fire-free station,
with \HCautFalse{} false refusals there (Wilson upper bound \HCautFalseUpper). A global $\tau=\KTauTen$ would also have stopped M0 in Q09.3
(descriptive, not registered; Supp.~Sec.~S7). Every paired mission is listed in Supp.~Table~S8.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
\textbf{When should a robot trust its memory?} Skipping a recent successful check is efficient, and its risk grows
with how often the world changes, which the robot cannot observe without looking (campaign H, Fig.~\ref{fig:hrate}; registered headline claim:
\HHeadline). Observation~1 and Corollary~1 make the question moot for safety: memory may decide the order and
strictness of the checks, never their existence, and buys time in persistent worlds at the price
of a detour when the world changes back.

\textbf{Moving beats thinking harder.} The largest single safety gain came from moving 0.4\,m (G), not from
a larger model, a better prompt or a second model (T2, whose fires were easy, cannot rank models on safety). A hidden fire carries no pixels to reason about.

\textbf{What pre-registration bought us.} Three registered claims failed or were not significant: false
approvals below 2\% on T1, a closed-loop benefit of better wording and crops (F), and any detectable change on T2 (too easy a draw). The first two exposed the hidden-fire weakness and led to the look-around;
unregistered, each could have been rationalised away. A check takes about \TimCheck\,s and the look-around about \GLookCost\,s, against about \TimDrive\,s of driving between
station and shelter, which is favourable where a wrong delivery costs a round trip.

\section{Limitations}
All results are from simulation: simple meshes, two supply types, fixed station geometry, one robot, a stylised fire, and sensor degradations rather than the appearance gap of real objects or smoke. Campaign H scripts its changes, assumes
independent changes, spans three missions and two stations, and does not compare memory caution with a global threshold at equal false-stop rate (Supp.~Sec.~S7). Corollary~1 assumes a per-check false-approval bound uniform over the stations a route meets.
Counterfactual baselines were not run physically, barely visible fires (21--613 pixels) are excluded, and the guard is hand-written, not formally synthesised~\cite{alshiekh2018shielding,ames2019cbf}.
The next step is a real platform. Threats to validity: Supp.~Sec.~S9.

\section{Conclusion}
Three lessons carry beyond our arena. First, an off-the-shelf local VLM can gate a physical action with few false
approvals if it is only asked to score closed-set answers, and if motion stays behind an independent guard (T1: \TvFA{} of
\TNdeny{} must-refuse images for v3.5; \RPoolFA{} under \RNcond{} camera degradations). Second, the failures that remained
were failures of viewpoint, not of the model: a fire hidden behind the supply was missed from the front and found from the
side (G: \GWrongB{} to \GWrongC{} of \GPairs). Third, memory should change \emph{where} and \emph{how carefully} a robot
looks, never \emph{whether} it looks; under this rule a robot gains speed from what it remembers while its risk bound
stays that of a robot without memory (H: \HHeadline). The two-model design is a registered claim reported in
Sec.~\ref{sec:res-jev} (J: \JClaim). All of this is simulation; the photo study shows that a site-specific threshold is
the first thing a real deployment must calibrate. Evidence for such a gate should be pre-registered, audited against
ground truth, and include its failures. We release code, worlds, test sets, protocols, ledgers and per-trial evidence;
every number here is produced from \texttt{experiments/results} by \texttt{scripts/paper\_numbers.py}.
