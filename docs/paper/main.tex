\title{Verify, Look Again, Remember: Fail-Closed Vision-Language Checks,\\Active Look-Around and Safety-Monotone Mission Memory\\for Relief-Supply Delivery Robots}
\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Anonymous Authors}\IEEEauthorblockA{Code, data and every trial log: \url{https://github.com/fe2131-art/rescue-mind}}}

\begin{abstract}
In disaster relief, the inventory a delivery robot is told to trust often disagrees with what is actually at
a supply point: items are swapped, shelves are empty, or the site has become dangerous.
We present \sys, a mobile delivery robot that stops at the supply station and checks the item
with a vision-language model (VLM) that runs on a local GPU, with no cloud service, before it carries anything to a shelter.
The check is built to fail closed. The VLM never generates free text for the decision. Instead, we read the next-token
probabilities of answer letters for two multiple-choice questions with permuted options and for a Yes/No hazard question.
The robot approves only if both questions agree with sufficient confidence and no fire or smoke is seen.
A wrong or missing item triggers a one-time search of the other station; a hazard stops the robot.
Motion stays behind an independent velocity guard that the VLM cannot command.
Every closed-loop trial is audited against simulator ground truth and a ROS bag. The held-out image tests and the closed-loop
campaigns were pre-registered, and every amendment is published.
On a pre-registered held-out set of \TNimages{} rendered images with unseen lighting, object placement and distractors,
the registered system (Qwen3.5-9B) approved \TFA{} of \TNdeny{} images that should have been refused (95\% CI \TFACI)
and \TCA{} of \TNallow{} that should have been approved. The supply check alone made \TSupplyFA{} false approvals in
\TSupplyN{} must-refuse images. Every false approval was a fire only partly visible behind the supply.
In \EN{} pre-registered closed-loop missions in freshly randomised Gazebo worlds, it behaved as expected in
\ECorrect{}/\EN{} trials with \EWrong{} wrong deliveries. A blind-delivery baseline would have made \EBzeroWrong{}.
To close the remaining gap, the robot also looks at the station from 0.4\,m to either side before it leaves.
In a paired, pre-registered campaign on new worlds with small, partly hidden fires, this reduced deliveries despite a
perceivable fire from \GWrongB{} to \GWrongC{} of \GPairs{} (exact McNemar $p=\GMcNemarP$), with no false stops.
A robot that resupplies the same shelter repeatedly can also remember what it saw. We let memory only reorder where the
robot looks and add caution, never remove a check. We show that this bounds the per-mission probability of an unsafe
delivery by the per-check false-approval rate, however stale the memory is. In a pre-registered campaign of \HPTwoN{}
paired missions in worlds that change between missions, the registered claim (memory's speed-up without its safety cost) was
\HHeadline. With relocated supplies, the median mission time went from \HTimeMzero\,s to \HTimeMtwo\,s. Unsafe deliveries
were \HUnsafeMone{} for a naive memory that trusts what it saw and \HUnsafeMtwo{} for ours. Contrastive zero-shot baselines
were either unsafe (SigLIP: \ZSigTOneFA{} false approvals) or unusable (CLIP: \ZClipTOneCA{} correct approvals). Under
\RNcond{} camera degradations the check made \RPoolFA{} false approvals. On real photographs it flagged \PRecall{} fire or
smoke images, but also \PFalseAlarm{} fire-free ones, so a site-specific threshold is needed before deployment. The original
two-model design runs on the simulated robot on one GPU (registered claim \JClaim), with Cloudflare's Clef-Flash, a
post-trained Qwen model, as the second judge.
We report the failure causes we found only by running the full stack, and release everything needed to reproduce the results.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Relief logistics is a ``last-mile'' problem: goods move from local depots to the people who need them
\cite{balcik2008lastmile}, under conditions in which records are stale and sites change by the hour
\cite{murphy2014disaster}. A robot that delivers whatever its inventory says is at a station may hand a
shelter a first-aid kit when it asked for water, report a delivery from an empty shelf, or, because an inventory
says nothing about hazards, drive into a fire. Vision-language models (VLMs) can often describe such situations
without task-specific training. Prompted language models explain manipulation failures after the fact
\cite{liu2023reflect}, fine-tuned VLMs detect or explain them \cite{duan2025aha,du2023successdetectors}, and run-time
monitors flag failures of learned policies \cite{agia2025sentinel}.
Their outputs, however, are biased by option order and answer letters \cite{zheng2024mcselectors,pezeshkpour2024order},
they hallucinate objects that are not there \cite{li2023pope}, and free-form generation can be unparseable.
We observed all three on our own images (Sec.~\ref{sec:findings}).

Relief robots also return: the same shelter is resupplied again and again, and a robot that remembers where the water
really was, or where it saw smoke, could be faster. Yet acting on stale memory can hurt: in a small navigation testbed, an agent that trusted unaudited memory failed more
often than one with no memory \cite{sun2026memorylies}, and a disaster site changes between missions. This raises a second question: \emph{how can
a robot benefit from memory without letting memory weaken its checks?}

We therefore ask a narrow, safety-motivated question: \emph{can an off-the-shelf VLM running on the robot
serve as a pre-action gate that rarely approves a wrong delivery, while still approving correct ones, and can
we show this with evidence a skeptical reader can audit?} Our answers are a system design and an evaluation
methodology:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A fail-closed checkpoint} (Sec.~\ref{sec:checkpoint}). Two multiple-choice questions with permuted
options plus a Yes/No hazard question are scored by answer-letter probabilities. The robot approves only if they agree
and clear a confidence threshold (which, on our held-out images, mattered only for weaker models). This turns the VLM into a selective classifier \cite{geifman2017selective}.
A recovery policy searches the other station after a wrong-item refusal but never after a hazard.
\item \textbf{Active look-around} (Sec.~\ref{sec:fix}). A fire hidden behind the supply is often plainly visible from the side.
After an approval the robot re-checks from two side poses, and any hazard there revokes the approval.
\item \textbf{Safety-monotone mission memory} (Sec.~\ref{sec:memory}). Memory proposes, perception disposes: what the
robot saw on earlier missions may reorder the stations it visits and tighten its hazard threshold, but never skip a check.
We state the invariant as a proposition, enforce it in code and in the run audit, and test it in a pre-registered
multi-mission campaign against a naive memory that trusts what it saw.
\item \textbf{The original two-model design on one GPU} (Sec.~\ref{sec:jev}). The original plan had two checkers, Qwen and a
second one called ``Jev''. We fill the Jev role with Clef-Flash, a decision model that Cloudflare post-trained from
Qwen3.5-9B \cite{cloudflare2026clefcard}, so the two models share lineage. Both must approve. We show how strongly
Clef-Flash's behaviour depends on how it is asked, and run the pair on the simulated robot.
\item \textbf{A separation of decision and motion in the spirit of Simplex} \cite{seto1998simplex,sha2001simplicity}.
The mission node only \emph{requests} velocities. An independent guard is the sole publisher of wheel commands and
stops on stale sensors, nearby obstacles or stale requests.
\item \textbf{Auditable, pre-registered evaluation} (Sec.~\ref{sec:method}). Prompts and thresholds were chosen
on development images only. We registered a 600-image held-out test set with unseen variation and the closed-loop
campaigns D, E, F, G, H and J before running them \cite{nosek2018preregistration,hofman2023preregistration}.
Each closed-loop trial is checked against Gazebo ground truth, a ROS bag and a recomputation of the decision.
Every attempt is kept, including aborted campaigns. Eleven studies (Table~\ref{tab:studies}) include a camera-degradation
study that probes part of the sim-to-real gap without a physical robot.
\item \textbf{Lessons from running the real stack} (Sec.~\ref{sec:findings}). There are \NFindings{} failure causes that unit tests and
mocks did not reveal, such as a wheel-axis frame error that made odometry-based audits pass a robot that never arrived.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Terms.} \emph{v3.4} is the first registered checkpoint (hazard threshold $\tauh=0.5$); \emph{v3.5} lowers it to $0.05$;
\emph{v3.6} additionally uses a hazard wording that names small, partly hidden fire and smoke and asks it on three enlarged
crops as well as on the full frame (Sec.~\ref{sec:fix}). A fire is \emph{visible} at a view if a rendered fire-only mask has at
least 614 pixels there, and \emph{perceivable} in a world if this holds in at least one of three views (front checkpoint,
left, right). A \emph{study} is any registered evaluation; a \emph{campaign} is a closed-loop study.
\emph{Expected behaviour} is the action the generator's ground truth requires in a trial: deliver, deliver after searching
the other station, or refuse because of a wrong item or a hazard. DH and DH2 are two development sets of hazard
images (Sec.~\ref{sec:method}).

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{Failure detection and verification with foundation models.}
REFLECT \cite{liu2023reflect} and AHA \cite{duan2025aha} explain manipulation failures in natural language
after the fact. Inner Monologue \cite{huang2023innermonologue} feeds success detection back into a planner.
Du et al.\ \cite{du2023successdetectors} fine-tune a VLM as a success detector.
Sentinel \cite{agia2025sentinel}, FAIL-Detect \cite{xu2025faildetect} and Code-as-Monitor \cite{zhou2025codeasmonitor}
monitor learned policies at run time, an LLM-based anomaly monitor triggers reactive fallbacks \cite{sinha2024realtime},
and uncertainty-aware policy steering decides when to act, ask or learn \cite{yuan2026whentoact}.
Semantic anomaly detection with LLMs \cite{elhafsi2023semantic} and visual question answering that abstains rather than
answers wrongly \cite{whitehead2022reliablevqa} are close in spirit to a gate that prefers refusal. Our check runs \emph{before} the robot acts and is closed-set and zero-shot. Its output is a
thresholded accept/reject that is audited against simulator ground truth.
The closest methodological relative is KnowNo \cite{ren2023knowno}, which scores multiple-choice answers by
next-token probability and asks a human for help, with a conformal-prediction coverage guarantee (see \cite{angelopoulos2021conformal} for background on conformal prediction).
SayCan \cite{ichter2023saycan} likewise scores a fixed candidate set by language-model likelihood.
We share the scoring, but we respond to uncertainty by refusing or searching, and we add permuted-option agreement as a
test-time defence against letter bias.

\textbf{Multiple-choice scoring and its biases.}
Scoring answer symbols works well when a model binds symbols to options \cite{robinson2023mcqa}. Language models are reasonably calibrated on
well-formatted multiple-choice questions \cite{kadavath2022know}, but they prefer certain option IDs \cite{zheng2024mcselectors} and orders
\cite{pezeshkpour2024order}, and their raw confidence needs validation \cite{guo2017calibration,zhao2021calibrate}.
Our two permuted questions are a minimal, deterministic form of self-consistency \cite{wang2023selfconsistency}.
The Yes/No hazard question follows the polling style of POPE \cite{li2023pope}.

\textbf{Safety architectures.} Simplex \cite{seto1998simplex,sha2001simplicity}, shields \cite{alshiekh2018shielding}
and control barrier functions \cite{ames2019cbf} keep a simple verified layer between a complex decision maker and
the actuators. LLM-controlled robots can be manipulated into harmful actions \cite{robey2025jailbreaking}, and
guardrails such as RoboGuard \cite{ravichandran2026guardrails} check plans before execution.
In contrast to vision-language-action models \cite{zitkovich2023rt2,kim2025openvla}, our VLM never commands motion.

\textbf{Memory for robots in changing environments.}
Robots increasingly keep long-horizon semantic memory. ReMEmbR \cite{anwar2025remembr} and Embodied-RAG
\cite{xie2024embodiedrag} retrieve from a database of captioned observations; KARMA \cite{wang2025karma} adds long- and
short-term memory to embodied agents, and H-EMV \cite{barmann2025hemv} verbalises a robot's episodic memory. 3D scene graphs such as Hydra
\cite{hughes2022hydra}, ConceptGraphs \cite{gu2024conceptgraphs} and HOV-SG \cite{werby2024hovsg} support planning
\cite{rana2023sayplan} and language grounding \cite{huang2023vlmaps}. Long-term mapping has long dealt with change
\cite{biber2005dynamic,krajnik2017fremen}, and recent work tracks changes in dynamic scene graphs
\cite{schmid2024khronos,behrens2025lostfound}. These systems use memory to \emph{answer} or \emph{plan}. A recent preprint reports, in a small grid-navigation testbed, that acting on
unaudited stale memory can be worse than having no memory \cite{sun2026memorylies}. GLOVE
\cite{yin2026glove} and VLM-DEWM \cite{tang2026vlmdewm} check memory or a world model against fresh observations to keep
the memory \emph{correct}.
We study a different use: memory decides \emph{where and how carefully to verify}. We restrict it so that it can never
reduce verification, and we state this as a provable invariant and test it under scripted change.

\textbf{Active perception.} Moving the sensor to resolve ambiguity is a classic idea \cite{bajcsy1988active,bajcsy2018revisiting},
and view planning for inspection is well studied \cite{scott2003viewplanning}. Recent embodied agents explore until a VLM
is confident \cite{ren2024exploreuntil} or let a VLM choose views \cite{sripada2024sceneexploration}. Our look-around is
deliberately simple: two fixed side views, used only to \emph{revoke} an approval. This makes its effect auditable and
its safety direction obvious.

\textbf{Robustness and the sim-to-real gap.} Image classifiers degrade under common corruptions
\cite{hendrycks2019benchmarking}, and so do detectors \cite{michaelis2019benchmarking}, adapted vision-language models
\cite{chen2023benchmarking} and video-language models \cite{schiappa2022robustness}. Sim-to-real transfer is a recognised challenge in robotics \cite{hofer2021sim2real}.
Without a physical platform, we measure one part of this gap, sensor degradation, under a pre-registered protocol. What
we care about is the \emph{direction} of the errors, not only accuracy.

\textbf{Diverse redundancy.} Requiring agreement between independently developed versions is a classic fault-tolerance
technique \cite{avizienis1985nversion}. Its benefit is limited by correlated failures \cite{knight1986experimental}, and
model-agreement schemes for language models have reappeared recently \cite{du2024debate}. We report both sides: the two
models agree on most images, and the second model's value depends strongly on how it is asked.

\textbf{Evaluation practice.} We follow recommendations to report conditions, multiple metrics, uncertainty
and failure modes \cite{kressgazit2024empirical}. We use Wilson intervals \cite{wilson1927probable} and exact McNemar
tests \cite{mcnemar1947note}. Domain randomisation \cite{tobin2017domain} is used here to build a held-out \emph{test} distribution, not for training.

\textbf{Platform.} A differential-drive robot with a 2D lidar and a mast camera (640$\times$480, 1.05\,rad
horizontal field of view) runs in Gazebo Harmonic with ROS~2 Jazzy \cite{macenski2022ros2,koenig2004gazebo}
(Fig.~\ref{fig:system}). The arena has two supply stations (water, medicine) and a shelter.
The VLM runs in FP16 on a single Tesla V100 (32\,GB) in the same container. We use Qwen3.5-9B \cite{qwen2026qwen35}
(Apache-2.0), selected over Qwen2.5-VL-3B \cite{bai2025qwen25vl}, Qwen3-VL-8B \cite{bai2025qwen3vl} and Qwen3.5-4B on
development data (Sec.~\ref{sec:method}).

\textbf{Mission.} A natural-language request (Japanese or English) is parsed into a supply and a
destination. The VLM answers two closed-set questions (supply: water / medicine / both / other; destination:
shelter~B / another place / unstated). The robot accepts the parse only under three conditions; otherwise it refuses
before moving:
\begin{itemize}
\item the most likely item is water or medicine with $p\ge 0.6$;
\item the most likely destination is not ``another place'' (shelter~B is the only shelter);
\item an explicit rule finds no other shelter name.
\end{itemize} It then drives to the station, aims the camera at the station centre, waits until odometry shows
it is stationary, and captures fresh frames whose ROS time, simulation clock and image quality are checked.

\subsection{Fail-closed checkpoint}\label{sec:checkpoint}
For an image $x$ and requested supply $s^\ast$, the VLM is asked two differently worded questions about what is on
the platform, with permuted options:
\begin{itemize}
\item $q_1$: A drinking water / B first-aid kit / C nothing or another object;
\item $q_2$: A neither (empty or a different object) / B first-aid kit / C drinking water.
\end{itemize}
It is also asked the hazard question $q_h$: ``Look at the whole image, including behind and around the objects.
Do you see any fire, flames, or smoke? Yes/No''. Each prompt asks for the letter (or Yes/No) only.
For each question we compute a softmax over the log-sum-exp of the next-token logits of each answer symbol's
tokenizer variants. This gives $p_k(s)$ for $k\in\{1,2\}$ and $p_h$. The robot approves iff
\begin{equation}
\bigwedge_{k=1,2}\Big[\arg\max_s p_k(s)=s^\ast \;\wedge\; p_k(s^\ast)\ge\taus\Big]\;\wedge\;p_h<\tauh,
\label{eq:rule}\end{equation}
with $\taus=0.5$ (fixed on development data and unchanged since the first registration) and $\tauh=0.05$.
The hazard question does not depend on the multiple-choice pass; it is asked once per image and reused.
In v3.6 it is also asked on three enlarged crops, and $p_h$ is the maximum over the full frame and the crops.
The hazard threshold was 0.5 up to campaign D. It was lowered once (v3.5) after a pilot missed a mostly occluded fire
(Sec.~\ref{sec:findings}). The asymmetry is deliberate: a missed fire can hurt people, while a false alarm only stops the robot.
Every failure mode leads to refusal: inference errors, non-finite logits (an FP16 overflow guard), unknown labels, images
with different hashes, or a model not on the allow-list.
A refusal is followed by a 2.5\,s zero-velocity hold that the audit checks.

\textbf{Recovery and hazard policy.} After a refusal because the item is wrong or missing, the robot checks the
other station once with the same rule. After a hazard refusal it never searches; it stops and reports.
A Japanese one-sentence situation report is generated for people but is never used for the decision.

\textbf{Velocity guard.} The guard publishes zero unless camera, lidar and odometry are fresh and the
front sector is clear. A stale \emph{motion} request latches the guard and ends the mission.
A stale \emph{zero} request holds zero without latching (Sec.~\ref{sec:findings}). A graph watcher triggers an
emergency stop if any other node publishes \texttt{/cmd\_vel}.

\subsection{Active look-around}\label{sec:lookaround}
A fire that burns behind the supply is occluded by the very object the robot is checking. A larger model cannot see
pixels that are not in the image, so the robot moves instead of thinking harder, in the spirit of active perception
\cite{bajcsy1988active,bajcsy2018revisiting}. After the front check approves, the robot drives to two poses 0.4\,m to
the left and right of the checkpoint, perpendicular to the line of sight. At each pose it aims at the station centre,
waits until stationary, captures fresh frames and asks the same questions. Only the hazard answer is used there, because
the supply may be out of view from the side. Any side view with a hazard turns the approval into a hazard refusal, and
the robot does not search (Fig.~\ref{fig:lookaround}). Formally, with side views $v\in V=\{L,R\}$, the approval becomes
$A = A_{\mathrm{front}} \wedge \bigwedge_{v\in V} [\,p_{h}^{(v)} < \tauh\,]$. The audit recomputes every side decision from
the saved outputs. In campaign G it added a median of \GLookCost\,s to a delivery without a hazard (from \GWallPlainB\,s to \GWallPlainC\,s
over the same fire-free worlds, Sec.~\ref{sec:fix}).

\subsection{Two models: the ``Jev'' role}\label{sec:jev}
The original design of this robot asked two models, ``Jev'' and Qwen, and moved only when both agreed. This
follows the old idea of diverse redundancy: independently built versions are unlikely to fail on the same input
\cite{avizienis1985nversion}, although their failures are known to be correlated in practice \cite{knight1986experimental}.
In our system the Jev role is played by Clef-Flash, a decision model released by Cloudflare \cite{cloudflare2026clefcard,chen2026clefblog}, served
locally through the same Jev request/response API. According to its model card \cite{cloudflare2026clefcard} it is
post-trained from Qwen3.5-9B. The two
models therefore share lineage. Their diversity lies in post-training, in model size (our on-robot pair uses
Qwen3.5-4B) and in the question asked, not in independent development, so the correlated-failure caveat of
\cite{knight1986experimental} applies in full. Its parsed answer gives a supply label with a confidence, a presence
probability and a hazard probability. The two-model rule approves only if Eq.~\eqref{eq:rule} holds for Qwen \emph{and}
Clef-Flash names the requested supply with confidence $\ge 0.65$, presence $\ge 0.80$ and hazard probability below its
own threshold. A hazard seen by either model stops the robot. A wrong supply seen by either model allows the one-time
search. The rule is deterministic, and the audit recomputes it. Whether the two models can share one GPU, and how the
second model should be asked, are empirical questions (Sec.~\ref{sec:res-jev}).

\subsection{Safety-monotone mission memory}\label{sec:memory}
After every mission the robot appends what \emph{it} observed to a memory file; ground truth is never written.
For each checked station the record holds:
\begin{itemize}
\item the supply both passes named with $p\ge0.5$, or \emph{uncertain};
\item whether a hazard was seen;
\item from which views (front, left, right) it was seen.
\end{itemize}
Before the next mission, memory may change exactly two things:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Route proposal.} The robot may visit first the station where it last saw the requested supply. This applies
when the supply's own station last showed something else. The station visited first is still checked, and the one-time
search still applies.
\item \emph{Caution.} At a station where a hazard was seen during the last three missions, every view refuses at
$p_h\ge\tau_h^{\mathrm{mem}}=0.01$ instead of $\tauh=0.05$. The side that revealed the hazard is looked at first.
\end{itemize}

Let $\theta=(\taus,\tauh,V)$ be the parameters of the check at a station, and let $A_\theta(o)\in\{0,1\}$ be its approval
for observations $o$ (all frames and model outputs at that station). Memory maps the history $\mathcal{H}$ to
$\theta'=\mu(\mathcal{H})$ and to a visiting order $\pi(\mathcal{H})$. We call a memory policy \emph{verification-preserving}
if, at every station it checks, $\taus'\ge\taus$, $\tauh'\le\tauh$ and $V'\supseteq V$. It must also approve a delivery
only through $A_{\theta'}$ at the delivering station.

\textbf{Observation 1 (per-check monotonicity).} For a verification-preserving policy, $A_{\theta'}(o)\le A_\theta(o)$
for every $o$. $A_\theta$ is a conjunction of a supply condition, which is non-increasing in $\taus$, and of hazard
conditions $[p_h^{(v)}<\tauh]$, which are non-increasing as $\tauh$ decreases. Adding views adds conjuncts, and the order of
the views does not change a conjunction. This is elementary, and it is meant to be: the point is that the invariant is
simple enough to enforce in code and to recompute in an audit.

The interesting question is what this implies for whole missions, because memory also changes \emph{which} stations are
checked. Let $\varepsilon$ bound the probability that the memory-free check $A_\theta$ approves a must-refuse view of a
station (a wrong or missing supply, or a perceivable fire). The bound must hold for every station view the robot can
encounter.

\textbf{Corollary 1 (per-mission risk).} A mission ends in at most one delivery, and a verification-preserving policy
delivers only after $A_{\theta'}$, and hence $A_\theta$, approved the delivering station. Therefore
$\Pr[\text{unsafe delivery}]\le\varepsilon$ for every visiting order $\pi$ and however stale $\mathcal{H}$ is. The memory-free
robot satisfies the same bound. A naive policy that delivers on $\mathcal{H}$ alone has no such bound: its risk is the
probability that the world changed since it last looked, which the robot cannot observe without looking.

Two caveats keep this honest:
\begin{itemize}
\item $\varepsilon$ is a property of the check on the views it meets. A different route meets different views, so the bound
transfers only to the extent that $\varepsilon$ is uniform over stations. We measure the per-check rates (E, G, T1, T2)
and the mission-level outcome (H) rather than assume them.
\item The corollary bounds risk, not time. A stale route proposal costs a detour, and Campaign H and the change-rate
model measure that cost.
\end{itemize}

\begin{algorithm}[t]\caption{One mission: verify, look again, search or stop, remember}\label{alg:mission}
\small\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require request $r$, memory $\mathcal{H}$
\State $s^\ast \gets$ parse$(r)$; \textbf{if} rejected \textbf{then} refuse before moving
\State $(\pi, \theta') \gets \mu(\mathcal{H}, s^\ast)$ \Comment{order of stations; $\tauh'\le\tauh$ at remembered hazards}
\For{station $k$ in $\pi$ (at most two)}
  \State drive to $k$; aim; wait until stationary; capture new frames $o_k$
  \State $d \gets$ \textsc{Check}$(o_k, s^\ast; \theta'_k)$ \Comment{Eq.~\eqref{eq:rule}; and the Jev-role model, if used}
  \If{$d=$ hazard} \State hold zero 2.5\,s; report; \textbf{stop} \EndIf
  \If{$d=$ approve}
    \For{side $v$ in order$(\theta'_k)$} \State move 0.4\,m to side $v$; capture $o_{k,v}$
      \If{$p_h(o_{k,v})\ge\tau'_{h,k}$} \State hold; report; \textbf{stop} \EndIf
    \EndFor
    \State deliver to the shelter; \textbf{break}
  \EndIf
  \If{$d=$ low confidence} \textbf{stop} \EndIf
  \State \Comment{wrong or missing supply: try the next station once}
\EndFor
\State $\mathcal{H} \gets \mathcal{H} \cup$ observations$(o)$ \Comment{what the robot saw, never ground truth}
\end{algorithmic}\end{algorithm}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:mission} summarises one mission.
A \emph{naive} memory is not verification-preserving. It replaces $A_\theta(o)$ by a predicate of $\mathcal{H}$
alone: it skips the check where it last saw the supply, and refuses without visiting where it last saw a hazard. We
implement it only as a baseline. The code enforces the premise: \texttt{apply\_memory\_caution} can only turn approvals into
refusals, and the run audit rejects any memory threshold above $\tauh$.

\section{Evaluation Methodology}\label{sec:method}
\textbf{Development data.} Prompts, the scoring method, thresholds and model choice were selected on 176 development images.
These are 48 with randomised viewpoints plus 128 with lighting presets, a cardboard-box distractor and fire.
The instruction parser was tuned on 40 development utterances. The later hazard fixes (Sec.~\ref{sec:fix}) also used two
hazard development sets of 300 rendered images each: DH, and DH2, which emphasises small early-stage fires.

\textbf{Held-out test sets.} \emph{Instructions:} 60 utterances frozen before the parser was built,
covering paraphrases, negation, lexical traps, other shelters and irrelevant requests.
\emph{Images (T1):} 60 Gazebo worlds $\times$ 2 stations $\times$ 5 viewpoints. These worlds use continuous random
lighting, supplies moved by up to $\pm$0.15\,m and freely rotated, random fire positions, and three distractors. Two of the
distractors never appeared during development: a blue crate, and an orange traffic cone (orange but not fire).
Fig.~\ref{fig:t1ex} shows examples. Labels come from the generator, never from a model. Whether the fire is visible is measured, not assumed. Each view of
a fire world is rendered a second time with only the flames and smoke drawn in flat magenta, so occlusion by the supply
or the platform is taken into account. A view counts as \emph{visible} with at least 614 fire pixels (0.2\%), as \emph{hidden}
with at most 20, and as \emph{ambiguous} in between. The \TNamb{} ambiguous images are excluded from the primary endpoints, as registered.

\begin{figure}[t]\centering\fig{fig_t1_examples.pdf}
\caption{Held-out T1 images (robot camera, 640$\times$480) with the generator's expected decision. The crate and the
traffic cone never appeared during development.}\label{fig:t1ex}\end{figure}
\textbf{Closed-loop campaigns.} Each trial starts a fresh Gazebo, bridge, guard, VLM server, watcher,
recorder and ROS bag. Campaign D (\DN{} trials, v3.4 with $\tauh=0.5$) used the three controlled scenes plus fire, dim light and a box.
Campaign E (\EN{} trials, v3.5) gives every trial its own randomised world, generated and registered before any E trial was run.
The strata are nominal, swapped, empty, fire at the requested station, and fire at the \emph{other} station, where the
search itself must stop.
A trial counts only if an audit confirms it:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Decision:} the decision is recomputed from the saved model outputs and image hash.
\item \emph{Arrival or stop:} the ground-truth trajectory (Gazebo odometry publisher) shows arrival at the shelter after approval, or no motion toward it after a refusal.
\item \emph{Odometry:} it agrees with ground truth.
\item \emph{Bag:} the ROS bag is finalised.
\end{itemize}
Only stack failures \emph{before} any VLM decision may be re-run once. All attempts stay in the ledger.

\textbf{Metrics and statistics.} The primary safety metric is the false-approval rate: approvals among
cases that must be refused because of a wrong or missing item or a visible fire.
It is paired with the correct-approval rate. We report Wilson 95\% intervals \cite{wilson1927probable}. Images of one world
share lighting, props and fire, so pooled rates also get a bootstrap interval that resamples whole worlds. Paired
comparisons on the same images or worlds use exact McNemar tests \cite{mcnemar1947note}. Paired times use the exact
Wilcoxon signed-rank test. A \emph{safe delivery} is possible when the requested supply is at one of the stations and no
fire burns there. In study R, \emph{pooled false approvals} sum the false approvals over all degraded images and conditions. A claim of improvement is made only if the pre-registered direction and $p<0.05$ both hold.
Otherwise we write ``not significant'' and report the counts.

\textbf{Labels for partly hidden fires.} Since T1, a fire is labelled by rendering the same world with only flames and
smoke drawn in flat magenta, at the actual camera pose (or, for world-level labels, at the front and both side poses).
A fire counts as \emph{visible} at a view with at least 614 pixels (0.2\% of the image), and as \emph{perceivable} in a
world if at least one of the front and the two side views shows it. Campaign F conditions on visibility at the checkpoint,
campaign G on perceivability. This label uses no model output,
and it is fixed before any trial.

\subsection{Fixing the remaining weakness}\label{sec:fix}
T1 left one failure mode: fires that are only partly visible behind the supply. Because T1 was then spent, candidate
fixes were compared on development data only. This included a new development set of small early-stage fires (DH2),
since the first hazard set (DH) contained just four visible fires under 10\,000 pixels. The candidates were:
\begin{itemize}
\item enlarged overlapping crops for the hazard question;
\item a hazard wording that names small, partly hidden or cut-off fire and smoke;
\item Clef-Flash (Sec.~\ref{sec:jev}), which answers the original plan's Jev request locally.
\end{itemize}
The wording together with the crops is v3.6.
Table~\ref{tab:i13dev} shows the results. The wording did most of the work: missed fires fell from \DevFAvThreeFive{} to
\DevFAB{} of \DevFireN{} when combined with crops, and correct approvals did not change. Clef-Flash refused half of the
correct deliveries when it was asked the original Jev questions, which concern the safety of a \emph{passage}. With
task-worded questions it reached 97\%, and agreement with Qwen gave the fewest false approvals. However, the two models
need 37\,GB together, which does not fit on one V100.

\emph{Held-out confirmation (T2, pre-registered, 600 new images).} The new draw happened to be easy: v3.5 already made

pixels at $p_h=0.019$.

\subsection{Camera degradations (study R)}\label{sec:res-r}
Gazebo renders are noise-free, sharp and well exposed. To probe this part of the sim-to-real gap
\cite{hofer2021sim2real} without a physical robot, we degraded the checkpoint frames of \RNimg{} held-out T2 images
(\RNworlds{} worlds) in \RNcond{} pre-registered ways. There are six families, in the spirit of ImageNet-C
\cite{hendrycks2019benchmarking} but with parameters in pixels of our camera: sensor noise, motion blur, defocus, under- and
over-exposure, and JPEG compression, each at a moderate and a severe level (Fig.~\ref{fig:rex}). A frame that the robot's own acquisition gate
rejects (black-out, white-out or flat frame) never reaches the VLM and counts as a refusal.
Table~\ref{tab:r} shows the results. Pooled over all conditions, the check made \RPoolFA{} false approvals (world-cluster
95\% CI \RPoolCI), so the registered fail-closed claim is \RPoolSupported. \RUnsafeConds{} conditions increased false
approvals significantly. Correct approvals went from \RCleanCA{} on clean frames to \RWorstCA{} in the
worst condition (\RWorstCond). The gate refused \RGateRej{} frames in total. At most \RFireMissMax{} visible fires were missed in any
condition. For a selective classifier the intended failure direction under shift is refusal rather than unsafe approval
\cite{geifman2017selective}; the counts above show how far this held.

\textbf{Real photographs (study P).} Rendered fire is stylised. We therefore also asked the frozen v3.6 hazard question about 400
real photographs from the test split of D-Fire \cite{venancio2022dfire}, a fire and smoke dataset released by its authors under CC0 (as stated in the original repository's LICENSE; we used a
Hugging Face mirror):
100 each with no fire, smoke only, fire only, and both. It flagged \PRecall{} of the photographs with fire or smoke (95\% CI
\PRecallCI) and \PFalseAlarm{} of the fire-free ones (\PFalseAlarmCI). The false alarms are not random. D-Fire's fire-free class
comes from fire-surveillance cameras and is rich in hard negatives: hazy hillsides, low clouds, a smudge on the lens, red
sunset clouds, glowing tents. Everyday scenes such as streets, beaches and roads scored $p_h\approx0.01$. The question was worded to
catch faint smoke (``a grey or black cloud'') and thresholded at 0.05, so it errs strongly toward stopping on real images.
This is the safe direction, but outdoors it would cost much of the availability. A site-specific threshold calibration on
local fire-free images is therefore a deployment prerequisite. We registered it as a follow-up study.

\subsection{Two AIs on one GPU (campaign J)}\label{sec:res-jev}
The second model is only as good as the question it is asked. Asked the original Jev request, which concerns whether a
\emph{passage} is safe, Clef-Flash approved half the correct deliveries on development data. Asked the task-worded
question, it approved 97\%. On the held-out T2 set, Clef-Flash with task wording alone made \TTClefFA{} false approvals
(\TTClefCA{} correct). Together with Qwen3.5-4B, the pair that fits on one 32\,GB V100, it made \TTPairFA{} (\TTPairCA{} correct).
Campaign J ran this pair on the robot, paired with v3.6 on 24 worlds that include swapped, empty and small-fire stations.
The two-model configuration (Qwen3.5-4B plus Clef-Flash) behaved as expected in \JCorrectJ{} runs (v3.6: \JCorrectA, exact McNemar $p=\JMcNemarP$), with
\JUnsafeJ{} unsafe deliveries (v3.6: \JUnsafeA) and \JFailedJ{} stack failures. Peak GPU memory was \JPeakVram\,GB, and
the median check latency was \JLatJ\,s for the two-model system and \JLatA\,s for v3.6. The registered claim that the original two-model design runs on the
simulated robot with no extra risk is \JClaim.

\section{Failure Analysis and Lessons}\label{sec:findings}
Running the full stack on a GPU revealed problems that \NTests{} offline unit tests and mocked ROS runs had not.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The auditor trusted the controller's sensor.} The wheel joint axis was expressed in the
child-link frame after a 90$^\circ$ roll, so the wheels spun about the vertical axis. Odometry reported 1.5\,m forward motion while the
body slid sideways by 0.75\,m. An audit based on \texttt{/odom} would have passed the run.
Audits now use Gazebo's ground-truth odometry. Changing the wheel collision from a cylinder to a sphere reduced
dead-reckoning drift from 0.16 to 0.011\,m.
\item \textbf{Free-form JSON from the first VLM was unusable} on our renders. With Qwen2.5-VL-3B, 21 of 48 outputs could not be parsed and the model hallucinated water on empty shelves.
A newer model parses reliably, but it still approves fewer correct deliveries than scoring does (Table~\ref{tab:t1models}).
This motivated closed-set scoring. T1 measures the free-JSON method again (Table~\ref{tab:t1models}).
\item \textbf{Middleware effects.} Best-effort QoS silently dropped 92 of 102 0.9\,MB camera frames under load. The GPU lidar
detected the robot's own camera mast. Slow DDS discovery on a 4-core host failed 4 of 12 starts in an aborted campaign.
An interrupted supervisor left an orphaned stack that made every trial of the next campaign stop with two
\texttt{/cmd\_vel} publishers. All of these are recorded with their fixes; the aborted campaigns are published.
\item \textbf{Stale-request latch.} Two pre-decision retries in campaign D happened while the robot was stationary: the mission node's
request stream stalled for $\approx$0.3\,s on the saturated CPU. We instrumented the mission loop, the guard
and garbage collection and rejected two hypotheses (guard starvation, GC pauses). Raising control-loop priority did not
remove the stalls. We therefore changed the guard: a stale zero request now holds zero, while a stale motion request still latches.
With a 0.6\,s stall injected before departure, the old guard failed 2/2 runs and the new one completed 2/2.
Raising priority with a file capability then had a side effect: it put the exec into the dynamic loader's secure mode,
which stripped \texttt{LD\_LIBRARY\_PATH} and broke the ROS bag recorder. A regression run caught this before any campaign.
\item \textbf{A pilot exposed a threshold that was not conservative enough.} Before campaign E we ran nine trials in
separate pilot worlds. In one, the robot found the wrong item, searched the other station, and delivered from it although a fire
burned behind it. The fire was almost entirely hidden by the first-aid kit, and $p_h=0.32$. On development data no fire-free
image exceeded $p_h=0.0007$, so we lowered $\tauh$ to 0.05 (v3.5). The same pilot worlds then gave 9/9 expected outcomes.
T1 was not involved: both thresholds were registered for T1 before any T1 image was scored.
\item \textbf{A generator bug caught by looking at the data.} In the first T1 render, distractors meant to stand next to a supply were placed through it. The
emptiness check ran after the supplies were moved. The render was discarded and the fix recorded as a
pre-registration amendment, before any model scored a T1 image. A second amendment replaced the field-of-view fire
label with the rendered-mask label, after we saw fires hidden behind supplies that the geometric label had called visible.
\end{itemize}

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
\textbf{When should a robot trust its memory?} The naive policy is not a straw man: skipping a check that succeeded a
few minutes ago is exactly what an efficiency-minded system would do. Campaign H and the change-rate model test how dangerous this is at a disaster site. Its risk should grow
with how often the world changes, which the robot cannot observe without looking (registered headline claim:
\HHeadline). Observation~1 and Corollary~1 give a rule that makes the question moot for safety. Memory may decide the order and the
strictness of the checks, never their existence. What memory may then buy is time in persistent worlds, at the price
of a detour when the world changes back.

\textbf{Moving beats thinking harder.} The largest single safety gain in our tests came from moving 0.4\,m (G), not from
a larger model, a better prompt or a second model. (T2, whose fires were easy, left no false approvals to remove, so it
cannot rank models on safety.) A fire hidden behind a box carries no pixels to reason about.

\textbf{What pre-registration bought us.} Beyond the outcomes of H, J and R above, three registered claims failed or were not significant:
\begin{itemize}
\item false approvals below 2\% on T1;
\item a benefit of better wording and crops in closed loop (F);
\item any measurable change on T2, whose fires happened to be easy (a power failure: no false approvals were left to remove).
\end{itemize}
Each failure redirected the work: it exposed the hidden-fire weakness and led to the look-around. Without registration,
each could have been rationalised away.

\textbf{Cost of verification.} A check takes about \TimCheck\,s and the look-around about \GLookCost\,s, against about \TimDrive\,s of driving between
station and shelter (Table~\ref{tab:timing}).
For relief logistics, where a wrong delivery costs a round trip and a delivery into a fire costs far more, this trade is
favourable. Memory recovers part of it when the world is persistent.

\section{Limitations}
All results are from simulation. The props are simple meshes, there are two supply types, the station geometry is fixed, and there is one robot.
The VLM sees rendered images. Study R covers sensor degradations, but not the appearance gap of real objects, real smoke
or clutter. Our ``hazard'' is a stylised fire. Campaign H scripts its changes, and the change-rate model assumes
independent changes. The memory has a fixed horizon of three missions and two stations, so a richer site would need a
spatial memory such as a scene graph. Corollary~1 assumes a per-check false-approval bound that holds uniformly over the stations a route meets.
The counterfactual baselines describe decision policies under identical observations; they were not executed physically.
Fire labels come from pixel counts. Barely visible fires (21--613 pixels) are excluded rather than adjudicated.
The guard is hand-written, not formally synthesised \cite{alshiekh2018shielding,ames2019cbf}.
The next step is a real platform with real supplies.

\section{Conclusion}
In our simulation experiments, a local VLM served as a pre-action gate for relief-supply delivery with few false approvals
(T1: \TFA{} of \TNdeny{} for v3.4 and \TvFA{} for v3.5, although the registered 2\% bound was not met) when its role was limited to
scored multiple-choice verification, motion was kept behind an independent guard and the robot looked again from the side.
Whether the gate stays fail-closed under camera degradation (R: \RPoolSupported), whether safety-monotone memory keeps its
speed-up without a safety cost (H: \HHeadline) and whether the two-model design runs without extra risk (J: \JClaim) are
registered claims reported in Sec.~\ref{sec:results}. Evidence for such a gate should
be pre-registered, audited against ground truth, and include its failures. We release the code, worlds,
test sets, protocols, ledgers and per-trial evidence.
