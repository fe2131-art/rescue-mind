\title{Verify, Search, or Stop: A Fail-Closed On-Site Check\\with a Local Vision-Language Model for Relief-Supply Delivery Robots}
\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Anonymous Authors}\IEEEauthorblockA{Code, data and every trial log: \url{https://github.com/fe2131-art/rescue-mind}}}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
In disaster relief, the inventory a delivery robot is told to trust often disagrees with what is actually at
a supply point: items are swapped, shelves are empty, or the site has become dangerous.
We present \sys, a mobile delivery robot that stops at the supply station and checks the item
with a vision-language model (VLM) that runs on a local GPU, with no cloud service, before it carries anything to a shelter.
The check is built to fail closed. The VLM never generates free text for the decision. Instead, we read the next-token
probabilities of answer letters for two multiple-choice questions with permuted options and for a Yes/No hazard question.
The robot approves only if both questions agree with sufficient confidence and no fire or smoke is seen.
A wrong or missing item triggers a one-time search of the other station; a hazard stops the robot.
Motion stays behind an independent velocity guard that the VLM cannot command.
Every closed-loop trial is audited against simulator ground truth and a ROS bag. The held-out image tests and the closed-loop
campaigns were pre-registered, and every amendment is published.
On a pre-registered held-out set of \TNimages{} rendered images with unseen lighting, object placement and distractors,
the registered system (Qwen3.5-9B) approved \TFA{} of \TNdeny{} images that should have been refused (95\% CI \TFACI)
and \TCA{} of \TNallow{} that should have been approved. The supply check alone made \TSupplyFA{} false approvals in
\TSupplyN{} must-refuse images. Every false approval was a fire only partly visible behind the supply.
In \EN{} pre-registered closed-loop missions in freshly randomised Gazebo worlds, it behaved as expected in
\ECorrect{}/\EN{} trials with \EWrong{} wrong deliveries. A blind-delivery baseline would have made \EBzeroWrong{}.
We report the 14 failure causes we found only by running the full stack, and release everything needed to reproduce the results.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Relief logistics is a ``last-mile'' problem: goods move from local depots to the people who need them
\cite{balcik2008lastmile}, under conditions in which records are stale and sites change by the hour
\cite{murphy2014disaster}. A robot that delivers whatever its inventory says is at a station will hand a
shelter a first-aid kit when it asked for water, report a delivery from an empty shelf, or drive into a
fire. Vision-language models (VLMs) can now recognise such situations zero-shot, and recent work uses them
to detect and explain robot failures \cite{liu2023reflect,duan2025aha,du2023successdetectors,agia2025sentinel}.
Their outputs, however, are biased by option order and answer letters \cite{zheng2024mcselectors,pezeshkpour2024order},
they hallucinate objects that are not there \cite{li2023pope}, and free-form generation can be unparseable.
We observed all three on our own images (Sec.~\ref{sec:findings}).

We therefore ask a narrow, safety-motivated question: \emph{can an off-the-shelf VLM running on the robot
serve as a pre-action gate that rarely approves a wrong delivery, while still approving correct ones, and can
we show this with evidence a skeptical reader can audit?} Our answers are a system design and an evaluation
methodology:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A fail-closed checkpoint} (Sec.~\ref{sec:checkpoint}). Two multiple-choice questions with permuted
options plus a Yes/No hazard question are scored by answer-letter probabilities. The robot approves only if they agree
and clear a confidence threshold. This turns the VLM into a selective classifier \cite{geifman2017selective}.
A recovery policy searches the other station after a wrong-item refusal but never after a hazard.
\item \textbf{A separation of decision and motion in the spirit of Simplex} \cite{seto1998simplex,sha2001simplicity}.
The mission node only \emph{requests} velocities. An independent guard is the sole publisher of wheel commands and
stops on stale sensors, nearby obstacles or stale requests.
\item \textbf{Auditable, pre-registered evaluation} (Sec.~\ref{sec:method}). Prompts and thresholds were chosen
on development images only. We registered a 600-image held-out test set with unseen variation and two
closed-loop campaigns before running them \cite{nosek2018preregistration,hofman2023preregistration}.
Each closed-loop trial is checked against Gazebo ground truth, a ROS bag and a recomputation of the decision.
Every attempt is kept, including aborted campaigns.
\item \textbf{Lessons from running the real stack} (Sec.~\ref{sec:findings}). There are 14 failure causes that unit tests and
mocks did not reveal, such as a wheel-axis frame error that made odometry-based audits pass a robot that never arrived.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{Failure detection and verification with foundation models.}
REFLECT \cite{liu2023reflect} and AHA \cite{duan2025aha} explain manipulation failures in natural language
after the fact. Inner Monologue \cite{huang2023innermonologue} feeds success detection back into a planner.
Du et al.\ \cite{du2023successdetectors} fine-tune a VLM as a success detector.
Sentinel \cite{agia2025sentinel}, FAIL-Detect \cite{xu2025faildetect} and Code-as-Monitor \cite{zhou2025codeasmonitor}
monitor learned policies at run time. Our check runs \emph{before} the robot acts and is closed-set and zero-shot. Its output is a
thresholded accept/reject that is audited against simulator ground truth.
The closest methodological relative is KnowNo \cite{ren2023knowno}, which scores multiple-choice answers by
next-token probability and asks a human for help, with a conformal guarantee \cite{angelopoulos2021conformal}.
SayCan \cite{ichter2023saycan} likewise scores a fixed candidate set by language-model likelihood.
We share the scoring, but we respond to uncertainty by refusing or searching, and we add permuted-option agreement as a
test-time defence against letter bias.

\textbf{Multiple-choice scoring and its biases.}
Scoring answer symbols works well when a model binds symbols to options \cite{robinson2023mcqa}. Language models are reasonably calibrated on
well-formatted multiple-choice questions \cite{kadavath2022know}, but they prefer certain option IDs \cite{zheng2024mcselectors} and orders
\cite{pezeshkpour2024order}, and their raw confidence needs validation \cite{guo2017calibration,zhao2021calibrate}.
Our two permuted questions are a minimal, deterministic form of self-consistency \cite{wang2023selfconsistency}.
The Yes/No hazard question follows the polling style of POPE \cite{li2023pope}.

\textbf{Safety architectures.} Simplex \cite{seto1998simplex,sha2001simplicity}, shields \cite{alshiekh2018shielding}
and control barrier functions \cite{ames2019cbf} keep a simple verified layer between a complex decision maker and
the actuators. LLM-controlled robots can be manipulated into harmful actions \cite{robey2025jailbreaking}, and
guardrails such as RoboGuard \cite{ravichandran2026guardrails} check plans before execution.
In contrast to vision-language-action models \cite{zitkovich2023rt2,kim2025openvla}, our VLM never commands motion.

\textbf{Evaluation practice.} We follow recommendations to report conditions, multiple metrics, uncertainty
and failure modes \cite{kressgazit2024empirical}. We use Wilson intervals \cite{wilson1927probable} and exact McNemar
tests \cite{mcnemar1947note}. Domain randomisation \cite{tobin2017domain} is used here to build a held-out \emph{test} distribution, not for training.

\section{System}\label{sec:system}
\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering\fig[\textwidth]{fig_system.pdf}

Ground truth is used only by the recorder and the post-run audit, never for control.}
\label{fig:system}
\end{figure*}
\textbf{Platform.} A differential-drive robot with a 2D lidar and a mast camera (640$\times$480, 1.05\,rad
horizontal field of view) runs in Gazebo Harmonic with ROS~2 Jazzy \cite{macenski2022ros2,koenig2004gazebo}
(Fig.~\ref{fig:system}). The arena has two supply stations (water, medicine) and a shelter.
The VLM runs in FP16 on a single Tesla V100 (32\,GB) in the same container. We use Qwen3.5-9B \cite{qwen2026qwen35}
(Apache-2.0), selected over Qwen2.5-VL-3B \cite{bai2025qwen25vl}, Qwen3-VL-8B \cite{bai2025qwen3vl} and Qwen3.5-4B on
development data (Sec.~\ref{sec:method}).

\textbf{Mission.} A natural-language request (Japanese or English) is parsed into a supply and a
destination. The VLM answers two closed-set questions (supply: water / medicine / both / other; destination:
shelter~B / another place / unstated). The robot accepts the parse only under three conditions; otherwise it refuses
before moving:
\begin{itemize}
\item the most likely item is water or medicine with $p\ge 0.6$;
\item the most likely destination is not ``another place'' (shelter~B is the only shelter);
\item an explicit rule finds no other shelter name.
\end{itemize} It then drives to the station, aims the camera at the station centre, waits until odometry shows
it is stationary, and captures fresh frames whose ROS time, simulation clock and image quality are checked.

\subsection{Fail-closed checkpoint}\label{sec:checkpoint}
For an image $x$ and requested supply $s^\ast$, the VLM is asked two differently worded questions about what is on
the platform, with permuted options:
\begin{itemize}
\item $q_1$: A drinking water / B first-aid kit / C nothing or another object;
\item $q_2$: A neither (empty or a different object) / B first-aid kit / C drinking water.
\end{itemize}
It is also asked the hazard question $q_h$: ``Look at the whole image, including behind and around the objects.
Do you see any fire, flames, or smoke? Yes/No''. Each prompt asks for the letter (or Yes/No) only.
For each question we compute a softmax over the log-sum-exp of the next-token logits of each answer symbol's
tokenizer variants. This gives $p_k(s)$ for $k\in\{1,2\}$ and $p_h$. The robot approves iff
\begin{equation}
\bigwedge_{k=1,2}\Big[\arg\max_s p_k(s)=s^\ast \;\wedge\; p_k(s^\ast)\ge\taus\Big]\;\wedge\;\max_k p_{h,k}<\tauh,
\label{eq:rule}\end{equation}
with $\taus=0.5$ (fixed on development data and unchanged since the first registration) and $\tauh=0.05$.
The hazard question does not depend on the pass, so $p_{h,1}=p_{h,2}$; it is asked once per image and reused.
The hazard threshold was 0.5 up to campaign D. It was lowered once (v3.5) after a pilot missed a mostly occluded fire
(Sec.~\ref{sec:findings}). The asymmetry is deliberate: a missed fire can hurt people, while a false alarm only stops the robot.
Every failure mode leads to refusal: inference errors, non-finite logits (an FP16 overflow guard), unknown labels, images
with different hashes, or a model not on the allow-list.
A refusal is followed by a 2.5\,s zero-velocity hold that the audit checks.

\textbf{Recovery and hazard policy.} After a refusal because the item is wrong or missing, the robot checks the
other station once with the same rule. After a hazard refusal it never searches; it stops and reports.
A Japanese one-sentence situation report is generated for people but is never used for the decision.

\textbf{Velocity guard.} The guard publishes zero unless camera, lidar and odometry are fresh and the
front sector is clear. A stale \emph{motion} request latches the guard and ends the mission.
A stale \emph{zero} request holds zero without latching (Sec.~\ref{sec:findings}). A graph watcher triggers an
emergency stop if any other node publishes \texttt{/cmd\_vel}.

\section{Evaluation Methodology}\label{sec:method}
\textbf{Development data.} Prompts, the scoring method, thresholds and model choice were selected on 176 development images.
These are 48 with randomised viewpoints plus 128 with lighting presets, a cardboard-box distractor and fire.
The instruction parser was tuned on 40 development utterances.

\textbf{Held-out test sets.} \emph{Instructions:} 60 utterances frozen before the parser was built,
covering paraphrases, negation, lexical traps, other shelters and irrelevant requests.
\emph{Images (T1):} 60 Gazebo worlds $\times$ 2 stations $\times$ 5 viewpoints. These worlds use continuous random
lighting, supplies moved by up to $\pm$0.15\,m and freely rotated, random fire positions, and three distractors. Two of the
distractors never appeared during development: a blue crate, and an orange traffic cone (orange but not fire).
Fig.~\ref{fig:t1ex} shows examples. Labels come from the generator, never from a model. Whether the fire is visible is measured, not assumed. Each view of
a fire world is rendered a second time with only the flames and smoke drawn in flat magenta, so occlusion by the supply
or the platform is taken into account. A view counts as \emph{visible} with at least 614 fire pixels (0.2\%), as \emph{hidden}
with at most 20, and as \emph{ambiguous} in between. The \TNamb{} ambiguous images are excluded from the primary endpoints, as registered.

\begin{figure}[t]\centering\fig{fig_t1_examples.pdf}
\caption{Held-out T1 images (robot camera, 640$\times$480) with the generator's expected decision. The crate and the
traffic cone never appeared during development.}\label{fig:t1ex}\end{figure}
\textbf{Closed-loop campaigns.} Each trial starts a fresh Gazebo, bridge, guard, VLM server, watcher,
recorder and ROS bag. Campaign D (\DN{} trials, v3.4 with $\tauh=0.5$) used the three controlled scenes plus fire, dim light and a box.
Campaign E (\EN{} trials, v3.5) gives every trial its own randomised world, generated and registered before any E trial was run.
The strata are nominal, swapped, empty, fire at the requested station, and fire at the \emph{other} station, where the
search itself must stop.
A trial counts only if an audit confirms it:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Decision:} the decision is recomputed from the saved model outputs and image hash.
\item \emph{Arrival or stop:} the ground-truth trajectory (Gazebo odometry publisher) shows arrival at the shelter after approval, or no motion toward it after a refusal.
\item \emph{Odometry:} it agrees with ground truth.
\item \emph{Bag:} the ROS bag is finalised.
\end{itemize}
Only stack failures \emph{before} any VLM decision may be re-run once. All attempts stay in the ledger.

\textbf{Metrics and statistics.} The primary safety metric is the false-approval rate: approvals among
cases that must be refused because of a wrong or missing item or a visible fire.
It is paired with the correct-approval rate. We report Wilson 95\% intervals. Paired comparisons on the same images use exact McNemar tests.

\emph{Where the errors are.} The supply check made \TSupplyFA{} false approvals on the \TSupplyN{} must-refuse images without
fire (\TSupplyFACI), including swapped and empty stations and unseen crates. All registered false approvals came from the
\TFireCorrectN{} images with the correct supply and a visible fire, \TFireCorrectFA{} of which were approved. In these
images the fire was partly hidden behind the supply, with only smoke or a flame edge in view. Detection depends on the visible
fire area: the hazard question flagged \TLargeDet{} visible fires of at least 10\,000 pixels but only \TSmallDet{} smaller ones.
The registered claim of fewer than 2\% false approvals is therefore \emph{not} supported: the Wilson upper bound is above 2\%.
Wilson intervals treat the images as independent, but the five views of a world share its lighting, props and fire.
A bootstrap that resamples the \TNworlds{} worlds gives \TFACluster{} for v3.4 and \TvFACluster{} for v3.5.
Table~\ref{tab:t1ablation} isolates the parts of the rule. Removing the hazard question raises false approvals to \TAblNoHazFA{}.
For the 9B model on these images, neither the permuted second question nor the confidence threshold changed the number
of false approvals. Their value shows up with a weaker model: for Qwen2.5-VL-3B, the second question alone approved
\TThreeBPassTwoFA{} must-refuse images and requiring agreement with the first question brought this down to
\TThreeBFullFA{}, which is the option-letter bias of \cite{zheng2024mcselectors} in action (Table~\ref{tab:t1ablation}).
\emph{Free-form generation.} Asking the same model for free JSON (A5) gave no parse errors with Qwen3.5-9B, the
same \TJsonNineFA{} false approvals, but only \TJsonNineCA{} correct approvals against \TCA{} with scoring. With
Qwen2.5-VL-3B, \TJsonThreeErr{} of the 1200 generations could not be parsed and \TJsonThreeCA{} images were approved.
All models missed largely the same small fires: hazard recall ranged from \THazMin{} to \THazMax{} of the \TNfireVis{} visible fires. Fig.~\ref{fig:riskcov} shows risk--coverage curves

\section{Failure Analysis and Lessons}\label{sec:findings}
Running the full stack on a GPU revealed problems that 506 offline unit tests and mocked ROS runs had not.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The auditor trusted the controller's sensor.} The wheel joint axis was expressed in the
child-link frame after a 90$^\circ$ roll, so the wheels spun about the vertical axis. Odometry reported 1.5\,m forward motion while the
body slid sideways by 0.75\,m. An audit based on \texttt{/odom} would have passed the run.
Audits now use Gazebo's ground-truth odometry. Changing the wheel collision from a cylinder to a sphere reduced
dead-reckoning drift from 0.16 to 0.011\,m.
\item \textbf{Free-form JSON from the first VLM was unusable} on our renders. With Qwen2.5-VL-3B, 21 of 48 outputs could not be parsed and the model hallucinated water on empty shelves.
A newer model parses reliably, but it still approves fewer correct deliveries than scoring does (Table~\ref{tab:t1models}).
This motivated closed-set scoring. T1 measures the free-JSON method again (Table~\ref{tab:t1models}).
\item \textbf{Middleware effects.} Best-effort QoS silently dropped 92 of 102 0.9\,MB camera frames under load. The GPU lidar
detected the robot's own camera mast. Slow DDS discovery on a 4-core host failed 4 of 12 starts in an aborted campaign.
An interrupted supervisor left an orphaned stack that made every trial of the next campaign stop with two
\texttt{/cmd\_vel} publishers. All of these are recorded with their fixes; the aborted campaigns are published.
\item \textbf{Stale-request latch.} Two pre-decision retries in campaign D happened while the robot was stationary: the mission node's
request stream stalled for $\approx$0.3\,s on the saturated CPU. We instrumented the mission loop, the guard
and garbage collection and rejected two hypotheses (guard starvation, GC pauses). Raising control-loop priority did not
remove the stalls. We therefore changed the guard: a stale zero request now holds zero, while a stale motion request still latches.
With a 0.6\,s stall injected before departure, the old guard failed 2/2 runs and the new one completed 2/2.
Raising priority with a file capability then had a side effect: it put the exec into the dynamic loader's secure mode,
which stripped \texttt{LD\_LIBRARY\_PATH} and broke the ROS bag recorder. A regression run caught this before any campaign.
\item \textbf{A pilot exposed a threshold that was not conservative enough.} Before campaign E we ran nine trials in
separate pilot worlds. In one, the robot found the wrong item, searched the other station, and delivered from it although a fire
burned behind it. The fire was almost entirely hidden by the first-aid kit, and $p_h=0.32$. On development data no fire-free
image exceeded $p_h=0.0007$, so we lowered $\tauh$ to 0.05 (v3.5). The same pilot worlds then gave 9/9 expected outcomes.
T1 was not involved: both thresholds were registered for T1 before any T1 image was scored.
\item \textbf{A generator bug caught by looking at the data.} In the first T1 render, distractors meant to stand next to a supply were placed through it. The
emptiness check ran after the supplies were moved. The render was discarded and the fix recorded as a
pre-registration amendment, before any model scored a T1 image. A second amendment replaced the field-of-view fire
label with the rendered-mask label, after we saw fires hidden behind supplies that the geometric label had called visible.
\end{itemize}

\section{Limitations}
All results are from simulation. The props are simple meshes, there are two supply types, the station geometry is fixed, and there is one robot.
The VLM sees rendered images whose domain gap to real cameras we have not measured. Our ``hazard'' is a stylised fire.
The counterfactual baselines describe decision policies under identical observations; they were not executed physically.
Fire labels come from pixel counts. Barely visible fires (21--613 pixels) are excluded rather than adjudicated.
The guard is hand-written, not formally synthesised \cite{alshiekh2018shielding,ames2019cbf}.
The next step is a real platform with real supplies.

\section{Conclusion}
A local VLM can serve as a fail-closed gate for relief-supply delivery when its role is limited to scored
multiple-choice verification and motion is kept behind an independent guard. Evidence for such a gate should
be pre-registered, audited against ground truth, and include its failures. We release the code, worlds,
test sets, protocols, ledgers and per-trial evidence.

\section*{Reproducibility}
One Dockerfile builds the full stack: ROS~2 Jazzy, Gazebo Harmonic, PyTorch, transformers. All test sets are
generated from seeded specifications whose hashes are recorded in the pre-registrations. All campaigns, including
the aborted ones, are in \texttt{experiments/results}. Every number in this paper is produced by
\texttt{scripts/paper\_numbers.py} from those files.
