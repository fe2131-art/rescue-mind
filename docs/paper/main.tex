\title{Verify, Look Again, Remember: Fail-Closed Vision-Language Checks,\\Active Look-Around and Safety-Monotone Mission Memory\\for Relief-Supply Delivery Robots}
\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Anonymous Authors}\IEEEauthorblockA{Code, data and every trial log: \url{https://github.com/fe2131-art/rescue-mind}}}

\begin{abstract}
In disaster relief, the inventory a delivery robot is told to trust often disagrees with what is actually at
a supply point: items are swapped, shelves are empty, or the site has become dangerous.
We present \sys, a mobile delivery robot that stops at the supply station and checks the item
with a vision-language model (VLM) that runs on a local GPU, with no cloud service, before it carries anything to a shelter.
The check is built to fail closed. The VLM never generates free text for the decision. Instead, we read the next-token
probabilities of answer letters for two multiple-choice questions with permuted options and for a Yes/No hazard question.
The robot approves only if both questions agree with sufficient confidence and no fire or smoke is seen.
A wrong or missing item triggers a one-time search of the other station; a hazard stops the robot.
Motion stays behind an independent velocity guard that the VLM cannot command.
Every closed-loop trial is audited against simulator ground truth and a ROS bag. The held-out image tests and the closed-loop
campaigns were pre-registered, and every amendment is published.
On a pre-registered held-out set of \TNimages{} rendered images with unseen lighting, object placement and distractors,
the registered system (Qwen3.5-9B) approved \TFA{} of \TNdeny{} images that should have been refused (95\% CI \TFACI)
and \TCA{} of \TNallow{} that should have been approved. The supply check alone made \TSupplyFA{} false approvals in
\TSupplyN{} must-refuse images. Every false approval was a fire only partly visible behind the supply.
In \EN{} pre-registered closed-loop missions in freshly randomised Gazebo worlds, it behaved as expected in
\ECorrect{}/\EN{} trials with \EWrong{} wrong deliveries. A blind-delivery baseline would have made \EBzeroWrong{}.
To close the remaining gap, the robot also looks at the station from 0.4\,m to either side before it leaves.
In a paired, pre-registered campaign on new worlds with small, partly hidden fires, this reduced deliveries despite a
perceivable fire from \GWrongB{} to \GWrongC{} of \GPairs{} (exact McNemar $p=\GMcNemarP$), with no false stops.
A robot that resupplies the same shelter repeatedly can also remember what it saw. We let memory only reorder where the
robot looks and add caution, never remove a check. We show that this bounds the per-mission probability of an unsafe
delivery by the per-check false-approval rate, however stale the memory is. In a pre-registered campaign of \HPTwoN{}
paired missions in worlds that change between missions, the registered claim (memory's speed-up without its safety cost) was
\HHeadline. With relocated supplies, the median mission time went from \HTimeMzero\,s to \HTimeMtwo\,s. Unsafe deliveries
were \HUnsafeMone{} for a naive memory that trusts what it saw and \HUnsafeMtwo{} for ours. Contrastive zero-shot baselines
were either unsafe (SigLIP: \ZSigTOneFA{} false approvals) or unusable (CLIP: \ZClipTOneCA{} correct approvals). Under
\RNcond{} camera degradations the check made \RPoolFA{} false approvals. On real photographs it flagged \PRecall{} fire or
smoke images, but also \PFalseAlarm{} fire-free ones, so a site-specific threshold is needed before deployment. The original
two-model design runs on the simulated robot on one GPU (registered claim \JClaim), with Cloudflare's Clef-Flash, a
post-trained Qwen model, as the second judge.
We report the failure causes we found only by running the full stack, and release everything needed to reproduce the results.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Relief logistics is a ``last-mile'' problem: goods move from local depots to the people who need them
\cite{balcik2008lastmile}, under conditions in which records are stale and sites change by the hour
\cite{murphy2014disaster}. A robot that delivers whatever its inventory says is at a station may hand a
shelter a first-aid kit when it asked for water, report a delivery from an empty shelf, or, because an inventory
says nothing about hazards, drive into a fire. Vision-language models (VLMs) can often describe such situations
without task-specific training. Prompted language models explain manipulation failures after the fact
\cite{liu2023reflect}, fine-tuned VLMs detect or explain them \cite{duan2025aha,du2023successdetectors}, and run-time
monitors flag failures of learned policies \cite{agia2025sentinel}.
Their outputs, however, are biased by option order and answer letters \cite{zheng2024mcselectors,pezeshkpour2024order},
they hallucinate objects that are not there \cite{li2023pope}, and free-form generation can be unparseable.
We observed all three on our own images (Supp.~Sec.~S1).

Relief robots also return to the same shelter again and again, and a robot that remembers where the water
really was, or where it saw smoke, could be faster. Yet acting on stale memory can hurt: in a FrozenLake-style grid world with six VLMs, an agent that trusted unaudited memory failed more
often than one with no memory \cite{sun2026memorylies}, and a disaster site changes between missions. So, \emph{how can
a robot benefit from memory without letting memory weaken its checks?}

We therefore ask a narrow, safety-motivated question: \emph{can an off-the-shelf VLM running on the robot
serve as a pre-action gate that rarely approves a wrong delivery, while still approving correct ones, and can
we show this with evidence a skeptical reader can audit?} Our answers are a system design and an evaluation
methodology:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A fail-closed checkpoint} (Sec.~\ref{sec:checkpoint}). Two permuted multiple-choice questions and a Yes/No hazard
question are scored by answer-letter probabilities; the robot approves only if they agree
and clear a confidence threshold, a selective classifier \cite{geifman2017selective}. A wrong item triggers a one-time search of the other station, a hazard never.
\item \textbf{Active look-around} (Sec.~\ref{sec:fix}). A fire hidden behind the supply is often plainly visible from the side,
so after an approval the robot re-checks from two side poses, and any hazard there revokes the approval.
\item \textbf{Safety-monotone mission memory} (Sec.~\ref{sec:memory}). Memory proposes, perception disposes: it may reorder the stations the robot
visits and tighten its hazard threshold, but never skip a check. We state the invariant, enforce it in code and in the audit, and test it in a pre-registered
multi-mission campaign against a naive memory.
\item \textbf{The original two-model design on one GPU} (Sec.~\ref{sec:jev}). We fill the second checker's role (``Jev'') with Clef-Flash,
which Cloudflare post-trained from Qwen3.5-9B \cite{cloudflare2026clefcard}, require both to approve, and run the pair on the simulated robot.
\item \textbf{Decision and motion separated, in the spirit of Simplex} \cite{seto1998simplex,sha2001simplicity}: the mission node only
\emph{requests} velocities, and an independent guard is the sole publisher of wheel commands.
\item \textbf{Auditable, pre-registered evaluation} (Sec.~\ref{sec:method}). Prompts and thresholds were chosen
on development images only; a 600-image held-out test set and the closed-loop
campaigns D, E, F, G, H and J were registered before running \cite{nosek2018preregistration,hofman2023preregistration}.
Each closed-loop trial is checked against Gazebo ground truth, a ROS bag and a recomputation of the decision,
and every attempt is kept. Eleven studies (Table~\ref{tab:studies}) include a camera-degradation
study that probes part of the sim-to-real gap.
\item \textbf{Lessons from running the real stack} (Supp.~Sec.~S1): \NFindings{} failure causes that unit tests and
mocks did not reveal, such as a wheel-axis frame error that made odometry-based audits pass a robot that never arrived.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Terms.} \emph{v3.4} is the first registered checkpoint (hazard threshold $\tauh=0.5$); \emph{v3.5} lowers it to $0.05$;
\emph{v3.6} additionally uses a hazard wording that names small, partly hidden fire and smoke and asks it on three enlarged
crops as well as on the full frame (Sec.~\ref{sec:fix}). A fire is \emph{visible} at a view if a rendered fire-only mask has at
least 614 pixels there, and \emph{perceivable} in a world if this holds in at least one of three views (front checkpoint,
left, right). A \emph{campaign} is a closed-loop study.
\emph{Expected behaviour} is the action the generator's ground truth requires: deliver, deliver after searching
the other station, or refuse because of a wrong item or a hazard.

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{Failure detection and verification with foundation models.}
REFLECT \cite{liu2023reflect}, AHA \cite{duan2025aha} and success detectors \cite{du2023successdetectors} explain or detect manipulation failures after the fact;
Sentinel \cite{agia2025sentinel}, FAIL-Detect \cite{xu2025faildetect} and Code-as-Monitor \cite{zhou2025codeasmonitor}
monitor learned policies at run time (more in Supp.~Sec.~S7). Our check runs \emph{before} the robot acts, is closed-set and zero-shot, and is audited against simulator ground truth.
The closest methodological relative is KnowNo \cite{ren2023knowno}, which scores multiple-choice answers by
next-token probability and asks a human for help with a conformal guarantee \cite{angelopoulos2021conformal};
SayCan \cite{ichter2023saycan} likewise scores a fixed candidate set. We share the scoring but respond to uncertainty by
refusing or searching, and add permuted-option agreement as a defence against letter bias
\cite{zheng2024mcselectors,pezeshkpour2024order,robinson2023mcqa}. Raw confidence needs validation \cite{kadavath2022know,guo2017calibration,zhao2021calibrate};
two permuted questions are a minimal self-consistency \cite{wang2023selfconsistency}, and the hazard question follows POPE \cite{li2023pope}.

\textbf{Safety architectures.} Simplex \cite{seto1998simplex,sha2001simplicity}, shields \cite{alshiekh2018shielding}
and control barrier functions \cite{ames2019cbf} keep a simple verified layer between a complex decision maker and
the actuators. LLM-controlled robots can be manipulated into harmful actions \cite{robey2025jailbreaking}, and
guardrails such as RoboGuard \cite{ravichandran2026guardrails} check plans first. Unlike vision-language-action models \cite{zitkovich2023rt2,kim2025openvla}, our VLM never commands motion.

\textbf{Memory for robots in changing environments.}
Semantic memory \cite{anwar2025remembr,xie2024embodiedrag,wang2025karma,barmann2025hemv} and 3D scene graphs
\cite{hughes2022hydra,gu2024conceptgraphs,werby2024hovsg} are used to \emph{answer} or \emph{plan}
\cite{rana2023sayplan,huang2023vlmaps}, and mapping has long dealt with change
\cite{biber2005dynamic,krajnik2017fremen,schmid2024khronos,behrens2025lostfound}. In a small grid-navigation testbed, acting on
unaudited stale memory can be worse than having none \cite{sun2026memorylies};
GLOVE \cite{yin2026glove} and VLM-DEWM \cite{tang2026vlmdewm} check memory against fresh observations to keep it \emph{correct}.
We instead use memory to decide \emph{where and how carefully to verify}, restrict it so that it can never
reduce verification, and test this invariant under scripted change.

\textbf{Active perception.} Moving the sensor to resolve ambiguity is a classic idea \cite{bajcsy1988active,bajcsy2018revisiting,scott2003viewplanning};
recent agents explore until a VLM is confident \cite{ren2024exploreuntil} or let it choose views \cite{sripada2024sceneexploration}. Our look-around is deliberately simple:
two fixed side views, used only to \emph{revoke} an approval.

\textbf{Robustness, redundancy and evaluation.} Classifiers, detectors and vision-language models degrade under common
corruptions \cite{hendrycks2019benchmarking,michaelis2019benchmarking,chen2023benchmarking,schiappa2022robustness}, and
sim-to-real transfer is a recognised challenge \cite{hofer2021sim2real}; we measure one part of this gap, sensor degradation, and care about the \emph{direction} of errors. Agreement between independently developed versions is a classic
fault-tolerance technique \cite{avizienis1985nversion} limited by correlated failures \cite{knight1986experimental}, with recent
model-agreement schemes \cite{du2024debate}. We follow recommendations to report conditions, metrics, uncertainty
and failure modes \cite{kressgazit2024empirical}, with Wilson intervals \cite{wilson1927probable} and exact McNemar tests
\cite{mcnemar1947note}; domain randomisation \cite{tobin2017domain} builds a held-out \emph{test} distribution here.

\textbf{Platform.} A differential-drive robot with a 2D lidar and a mast camera (640$\times$480, 1.05\,rad
horizontal field of view) runs in Gazebo Harmonic with ROS~2 Jazzy \cite{macenski2022ros2,koenig2004gazebo}
(Fig.~\ref{fig:system}). The arena has two supply stations (water, medicine) and a shelter.
The VLM runs in FP16 on a single Tesla V100 (32\,GB). We use Qwen3.5-9B \cite{qwen2026qwen35}
(Apache-2.0), selected over Qwen2.5-VL-3B \cite{bai2025qwen25vl}, Qwen3-VL-8B \cite{bai2025qwen3vl} and Qwen3.5-4B on
development data (Sec.~\ref{sec:method}).

\textbf{Mission.} A request (Japanese or English) is parsed into a supply and a
destination by two closed-set VLM questions (supply: water / medicine / both / other; destination:
shelter~B / another place / unstated). The robot accepts the parse only if the most likely item is water or medicine with $p\ge 0.6$,
the most likely destination is not ``another place'' (shelter~B is the only shelter), and an explicit rule finds no other
shelter name; otherwise it refuses before moving. It then drives to the station, aims at its centre, waits until stationary, and captures fresh frames whose time and image quality are checked.

\subsection{Fail-closed checkpoint}\label{sec:checkpoint}
For an image $x$ and requested supply $s^\ast$, the VLM is asked two differently worded questions about what is on
the platform, with permuted options:
\begin{itemize}
\item $q_1$: A drinking water / B first-aid kit / C nothing or another object;
\item $q_2$: A neither (empty or a different object) / B first-aid kit / C drinking water.
\end{itemize}
It is also asked the hazard question $q_h$: ``Look at the whole image, including behind and around the objects.
Do you see any fire, flames, or smoke? Yes/No''. Each prompt asks for the letter (or Yes/No) only.
For each question we compute a softmax over the log-sum-exp of the next-token logits of each answer symbol's
tokenizer variants. This gives $p_k(s)$ for $k\in\{1,2\}$ and $p_h$. The robot approves iff
\begin{equation}
\bigwedge_{k=1,2}\Big[\arg\max_s p_k(s)=s^\ast \;\wedge\; p_k(s^\ast)\ge\taus\Big]\;\wedge\;p_h<\tauh,
\label{eq:rule}\end{equation}
with $\taus=0.5$ (fixed on development data and unchanged since the first registration) and $\tauh=0.05$.
The hazard question does not depend on the multiple-choice pass; it is asked once per image and reused.
In v3.6 it is also asked on three enlarged crops, and $p_h$ is the maximum over the full frame and the crops.
The hazard threshold was 0.5 up to campaign D and was lowered once (v3.5) after a pilot missed a mostly occluded fire
(Supp.~Sec.~S1). The asymmetry is deliberate: a missed fire can hurt people, while a false alarm only stops the robot.
Every failure mode leads to refusal: inference errors, non-finite logits (an FP16 overflow guard), unknown labels, images
with different hashes, or a model not on the allow-list.
A refusal is followed by a 2.5\,s zero-velocity hold that the audit checks.

\textbf{Recovery and hazard policy.} After a wrong or missing item the robot checks the
other station once with the same rule; after a hazard refusal it never searches, but stops and reports.

\textbf{Velocity guard.} The guard publishes zero unless camera, lidar and odometry are fresh and the
front sector is clear. A stale \emph{motion} request latches the guard and ends the mission.
A stale \emph{zero} request holds zero without latching (Supp.~Sec.~S1). A graph watcher triggers an
emergency stop if any other node publishes \texttt{/cmd\_vel}.

\subsection{Active look-around}\label{sec:lookaround}
A fire behind the supply is occluded by the very object the robot is checking. A larger model cannot see
pixels that are not in the image, so the robot moves instead of thinking harder, in the spirit of active perception
\cite{bajcsy1988active,bajcsy2018revisiting}. After the front check approves, the robot drives to two poses 0.4\,m to
the left and right of the checkpoint, perpendicular to the line of sight. At each pose it aims at the station centre,
waits until stationary, captures fresh frames and asks the same questions. Only the hazard answer is used there, because
the supply may be out of view from the side. Any side view with a hazard turns the approval into a hazard refusal, with
no search (Fig.~\ref{fig:lookaround}). Formally, with side views $v\in V=\{L,R\}$, the approval becomes
$A = A_{\mathrm{front}} \wedge \bigwedge_{v\in V} [\,p_{h}^{(v)} < \tauh\,]$. The audit recomputes every side decision from
the saved outputs. In campaign G it added a median of \GLookCost\,s to a delivery without a hazard (from \GWallPlainB\,s to \GWallPlainC\,s
over the same fire-free worlds, Sec.~\ref{sec:fix}).

\subsection{Two models: the ``Jev'' role}\label{sec:jev}
The original design of this robot asked two models, ``Jev'' and Qwen, and moved only when both agreed, following the
idea of diverse redundancy \cite{avizienis1985nversion}, although failures are known to be correlated in practice
\cite{knight1986experimental}. The Jev role is played by Clef-Flash, a decision model released by Cloudflare
\cite{cloudflare2026clefcard,chen2026clefblog} and served locally through the same Jev request/response API.
According to its model card it is post-trained from Qwen3.5-9B, so the two models share lineage. Their diversity lies in
post-training, in model size (our on-robot pair uses Qwen3.5-4B) and in the question asked, and the correlated-failure
caveat applies in full. The two-model rule approves only if Eq.~\eqref{eq:rule} holds for Qwen \emph{and}
Clef-Flash agrees (thresholds: Supp.~Sec.~S8). A hazard seen by either model stops the robot, and a wrong supply seen by either
allows the one-time search. The rule is deterministic, and the audit recomputes it. Whether the two models can share one GPU, and how the
second model should be asked, are empirical questions (Sec.~\ref{sec:res-jev}).

\subsection{Safety-monotone mission memory}\label{sec:memory}
After every mission the robot appends what \emph{it} observed to a memory file; ground truth is never written.
For each checked station the record holds:
\begin{itemize}
\item the supply both passes named with $p\ge0.5$, or \emph{uncertain};
\item whether a hazard was seen;
\item from which views (front, left, right) it was seen.
\end{itemize}
Before the next mission, memory may change exactly two things:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Route proposal.} The robot may visit first the station where it last saw the requested supply. This applies
when the supply's own station last showed something else. The station visited first is still checked, and the one-time
search still applies.
\item \emph{Caution.} At a station where a hazard was seen during the last three missions, every view refuses at
$p_h\ge\tau_h^{\mathrm{mem}}=0.01$ instead of $\tauh=0.05$. The side that revealed the hazard is looked at first.
\end{itemize}

Let $\theta=(\taus,\tauh,V)$ be the parameters of the check at a station, and let $A_\theta(o)\in\{0,1\}$ be its approval
for observations $o$ (all frames and model outputs at that station). Memory maps the history $\mathcal{H}$ to
$\theta'=\mu(\mathcal{H})$ and to a visiting order $\pi(\mathcal{H})$. We call a memory policy \emph{verification-preserving}
if, at every station it checks, $\taus'\ge\taus$, $\tauh'\le\tauh$ and $V'\supseteq V$. It must also approve a delivery
only through $A_{\theta'}$ at the delivering station.

\textbf{Observation 1 (per-check monotonicity).} For a verification-preserving policy, $A_{\theta'}(o)\le A_\theta(o)$
for every $o$. $A_\theta$ is a conjunction of a supply condition, which is non-increasing in $\taus$, and of hazard
conditions $[p_h^{(v)}<\tauh]$, which are non-increasing as $\tauh$ decreases. Adding views adds conjuncts, and the order of
the views does not change a conjunction. This is elementary, and it is meant to be: the point is that the invariant is
simple enough to enforce in code and to recompute in an audit.

The interesting question is what this implies for whole missions, because memory also changes \emph{which} stations are
checked. Let $\varepsilon$ bound the probability that the memory-free check $A_\theta$ approves a must-refuse view of a
station (a wrong or missing supply, or a perceivable fire). The bound must hold for every station view the robot can
encounter.

\textbf{Corollary 1 (per-mission risk).} A mission ends in at most one delivery, and a verification-preserving policy
delivers only after $A_{\theta'}$, and hence $A_\theta$, approved the delivering station. Therefore
$\Pr[\text{unsafe delivery}]\le\varepsilon$ for every visiting order $\pi$ and however stale $\mathcal{H}$ is. The memory-free
robot satisfies the same bound. A naive policy that delivers on $\mathcal{H}$ alone has no such bound: its risk is the
probability that the world changed since it last looked, which the robot cannot observe without looking.

Two caveats keep this honest:
\begin{itemize}
\item $\varepsilon$ is a property of the check on the views it meets. A different route meets different views, so the bound
transfers only to the extent that $\varepsilon$ is uniform over stations. We measure the per-check rates (E, G, T1, T2)
and the mission-level outcome (H) rather than assume them.
\item The corollary bounds risk, not time. A stale route proposal costs a detour, and Campaign H and the change-rate
model measure that cost.
\end{itemize}

\begin{algorithm}[t]\caption{One mission: verify, look again, search or stop, remember}\label{alg:mission}
\small\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require request $r$, memory $\mathcal{H}$
\State $s^\ast \gets$ parse$(r)$; \textbf{if} rejected \textbf{then} refuse before moving
\State $(\pi, \theta') \gets \mu(\mathcal{H}, s^\ast)$ \Comment{order of stations; $\tauh'\le\tauh$ at remembered hazards}
\For{station $k$ in $\pi$ (at most two)}
  \State drive to $k$; aim; wait until stationary; capture new frames $o_k$
  \State $d \gets$ \textsc{Check}$(o_k, s^\ast; \theta'_k)$ \Comment{Eq.~\eqref{eq:rule}; and the Jev-role model, if used}
  \If{$d=$ hazard} \State hold zero 2.5\,s; report; \textbf{stop} \EndIf
  \If{$d=$ approve}
    \For{side $v$ in order$(\theta'_k)$} \State move 0.4\,m to side $v$; capture $o_{k,v}$
      \If{$p_h(o_{k,v})\ge\tau'_{h,k}$} \State hold; report; \textbf{stop} \EndIf
    \EndFor
    \State deliver to the shelter; \textbf{break}
  \EndIf
  \If{$d=$ low confidence} \textbf{stop} \EndIf
  \State \Comment{wrong or missing supply: try the next station once}
\EndFor
\State $\mathcal{H} \gets \mathcal{H} \cup$ observations$(o)$ \Comment{what the robot saw, never ground truth}
\end{algorithmic}\end{algorithm}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:mission} summarises one mission.
A \emph{naive} memory is not verification-preserving. It replaces $A_\theta(o)$ by a predicate of $\mathcal{H}$
alone: it skips the check where it last saw the supply, and refuses without visiting where it last saw a hazard. We
implement it only as a baseline. The code enforces the premise: \texttt{apply\_memory\_caution} can only turn approvals into
refusals, and the run audit rejects any memory threshold above $\tauh$.

\section{Evaluation Methodology}\label{sec:method}
\textbf{Development data.} Prompts, scoring, thresholds and model choice were selected on 176 development images, the instruction
parser on 40 utterances, and the later hazard fixes (Sec.~\ref{sec:fix}) also on two 300-image hazard sets, DH and DH2
(composition: Supp.~Sec.~S8).

\textbf{Held-out test sets.} \emph{Instructions:} 60 utterances frozen before the parser was built,
covering paraphrases, negation, lexical traps, other shelters and irrelevant requests.
\emph{Images (T1):} 60 Gazebo worlds $\times$ 2 stations $\times$ 5 viewpoints, with continuous random
lighting, supplies moved by up to $\pm$0.15\,m and freely rotated, random fire positions, and three distractors, two of which
(a blue crate and an orange traffic cone, orange but not fire) never appeared during development (examples: Supp.~Fig.~S3).
Labels come from the generator, never from a model. Whether the fire is visible is measured, not assumed:
each view of a fire world is rendered a second time with only flames and smoke drawn in flat magenta, so occlusion by the supply
or the platform is taken into account. A view is \emph{hidden}
with at most 20 fire pixels and \emph{ambiguous} with 21--613; the \TNamb{} ambiguous images are excluded from the primary endpoints, as registered.
Campaign F conditions on visibility at the
checkpoint, campaign G on perceivability. These labels use no model output and are fixed before any trial.

\textbf{Closed-loop campaigns.} Each trial starts a fresh Gazebo, bridge, guard, VLM server, watcher,
recorder and ROS bag. Campaign D (\DN{} trials, v3.4 with $\tauh=0.5$) used the three controlled scenes plus fire, dim light and a box.
Campaign E (\EN{} trials, v3.5) gives every trial its own randomised world, registered before any E trial was run, in the strata
nominal, swapped, empty, fire at the requested station, and fire at the \emph{other} station, where the
search itself must stop.
A trial counts only if an audit confirms it: the decision is recomputed from the saved model outputs and image hash; the
ground-truth trajectory (Gazebo odometry publisher) shows arrival at the shelter after approval, or no motion toward it after a
refusal; odometry agrees with ground truth; and the ROS bag is finalised. Only stack failures \emph{before} any VLM decision may be re-run once. All attempts stay in the ledger.

\textbf{Metrics and statistics.} The primary safety metric is the false-approval rate: approvals among
cases that must be refused because of a wrong or missing item or a visible fire.
It is paired with the correct-approval rate. We report Wilson 95\% intervals \cite{wilson1927probable}; images of one world
share lighting, props and fire, so pooled rates also get a bootstrap interval that resamples whole worlds. Paired
comparisons use exact McNemar tests \cite{mcnemar1947note} and, for times, the exact
Wilcoxon signed-rank test. A \emph{safe delivery} is possible when the requested supply is at one of the stations and no
fire burns there. In study R, \emph{pooled false approvals} sum the false approvals over all degraded images and conditions. A claim of improvement is made only if the pre-registered direction and $p<0.05$ both hold;
otherwise we write ``not significant'' and report the counts.

\textbf{Model selection and registration audit.} Qwen3.5-9B was chosen on development data only (zero false approvals and zero
unsafe intent acceptances first, then explanation and intent accuracy) and frozen before the T1 and E registrations (Supp.~Sec.~S5).
For every study with results, the registration commit precedes the results commit (\PreregOrderOK), except campaign C (Supp.~Sec.~S6, Table~S5).

\subsection{Closed-loop missions}
In campaign D the system behaved as expected in \DCorrect{}/\DN{} trials (\DCorrectCI) with \DWrong{} wrong deliveries
(median decision latency \DLatMed\,s, maximum \DLatMax\,s; \DRetries{} pre-decision infrastructure retries).
In campaign E, with a new random world in every trial, it behaved as expected in \ECorrect{}/\EN{} (\ECorrectCI) with

\emph{Counterfactual baselines on the same trials:} blind delivery (B0), which trusts the inventory, would have made \EBzeroWrong{} wrong

\emph{Time.} In the \TimingN{} delivered missions without search, the check takes about \TimCheck\,s (scored VLM queries:
\TimVlm\,s) and driving between station and shelter about \TimDrive\,s; the phase breakdown is in Supp.~Table~S1.

\subsection{Fixing the remaining weakness}\label{sec:fix}
T1 left one failure mode: fires only partly visible behind the supply. Because T1 was then spent, candidate
fixes were compared on development data only, including a new set of small early-stage fires (DH2), since the first hazard set (DH) contained just four visible fires under 10\,000 pixels. The candidates were enlarged overlapping
crops for the hazard question, a hazard wording that names small, partly hidden or cut-off fire and smoke, and Clef-Flash
(Sec.~\ref{sec:jev}). The wording together with the crops is v3.6.
Supp.~Table~S7 shows the results. The wording did most of the work: missed fires fell from \DevFAvThreeFive{} to
\DevFAB{} of \DevFireN{} when combined with crops, and correct approvals did not change. Clef-Flash refused half of the
correct deliveries when asked the original Jev questions, which concern the safety of a \emph{passage}; with
task-worded questions it reached 97\%, and agreement with Qwen gave the fewest false approvals, but the two models
need 37\,GB together, which does not fit on one V100.

\emph{Held-out confirmation (T2, pre-registered, 600 new images).} The new draw happened to be easy: v3.5 already made
\TTRefFA{} false approvals (\TTRefCA{} correct approvals), so no configuration could improve on it. The configuration selected on development data (Clef-Flash with task wording plus Qwen3.5-9B v3.6, 37\,GB) made
\TTSelFA{} false approvals with \TTSelCA{} correct approvals, and Qwen3.5-9B v3.6 alone made \TTBFA{} with \TTBCA{}.
Clef-Flash \emph{alone}, given the task-worded questions, made \TTClefFA{} false approvals with
\TTClefCA{} correct approvals in a single forward pass (median 0.6\,s per image, against \TimVlm\,s for the scored
Qwen queries; Supp.~Table~S1).

\emph{Closed loop: small fires (campaign F, pre-registered, paired).} Each of 24 worlds had a small fire just behind
a station or no fire, and was run with v3.5 and with v3.6. In the \FPairs{} worlds where the fire
was visible at the checkpoint in both runs, v3.5 delivered despite the fire \FWrongA{} times and v3.6 \FWrongB{} times (exact
McNemar $p=\FMcNemarP$, not significant; \FDiscA{} discordant worlds favoured v3.6 and \FDiscB{} the other).
Neither system stopped falsely in the fire-free runs (v3.5 \FFalseStopA{}, v3.6 \FFalseStopB{}), which with so few runs says
little about the false-stop rate. Six of v3.6's seven remaining misses were small fires whose hazard probabilities overlap
those of fire-free views (Supp.~Sec.~S9).

\subsection{Camera degradations (study R)}\label{sec:res-r}
Gazebo renders are noise-free and well exposed. To probe this part of the sim-to-real gap
\cite{hofer2021sim2real}, we degraded the checkpoint frames of \RNimg{} held-out T2 images
(\RNworlds{} worlds) in \RNcond{} pre-registered ways. There are six families, in the spirit of ImageNet-C
\cite{hendrycks2019benchmarking}: sensor noise, motion blur, defocus, under- and
over-exposure, and JPEG compression, each at a moderate and a severe level (Fig.~\ref{fig:rex}). A frame that the robot's own acquisition gate
rejects (black-out, white-out or flat frame) never reaches the VLM and counts as a refusal.
Supp.~Table~S6 gives every condition. Pooled over all conditions, the check made \RPoolFA{} false approvals (world-cluster
95\% CI \RPoolCI), so the registered fail-closed claim is \RPoolSupported. \RUnsafeConds{} conditions increased false
approvals significantly. Correct approvals went from \RCleanCA{} on clean frames to \RWorstCA{} in the
worst condition (\RWorstCond). The gate refused \RGateRej{} frames in total, and at most \RFireMissMax{} visible fires were missed in any
condition. Under shift, refusal rather than unsafe approval is the intended failure direction of a selective classifier
\cite{geifman2017selective}.

\textbf{Real photographs (study P).} We also asked the frozen v3.6 hazard question about 400
real photographs from the test split of D-Fire \cite{venancio2022dfire}, a CC0 fire and smoke dataset
(100 each with no fire, smoke only, fire only, and both). It flagged \PRecall{} of the photographs with fire or smoke (95\% CI
\PRecallCI) and \PFalseAlarm{} of the fire-free ones (\PFalseAlarmCI); the false alarms concentrate on hard negatives such as hazy
hillsides and sunset clouds (Supp.~Sec.~S9). The question thus errs strongly toward stopping on real images, which is
the safe direction but would cost availability outdoors, so a site-specific threshold calibration is a deployment prerequisite.
Study P2 tested this: it chose wording and $\tau_h$ on 400 separate calibration photographs
(recall $\ge 0.95$ first, then the lower false-alarm rate), with wording \PTwoWording{} and $\tau_h=\PTwoTau$, and applied it to 400 test photographs.
It flagged \PTwoRecall{} of
those with fire or smoke (\PTwoRecallCI) and \PTwoFA{} of the fire-free ones (\PTwoFACI), against \PTwoFrozenRecall{} and
\PTwoFrozenFA{} for the frozen $\tau_h=0.05$. The registered claim (test recall $\ge 0.90$ and false-alarm rate $\le 0.20$) was \PTwoClaim{}.

\subsection{Cross-mission memory (campaign H)}\label{sec:res-h}
Campaign H has 16 sequences of three missions to the same shelter. Lighting and prop placement are re-drawn each mission,

The first mission seeds all three arms; missions 2 and 3 run with no memory (M0, v3.6), naive memory (M1) and verified
memory (M2). Table~\ref{tab:h} (per-mission outcomes and times: Supp.~Fig.~S4) summarises the \HPTwoN{} paired missions. On the \HPOneN{} missions whose supplies stayed relocated, M2 went
straight to the right station, with a median mission time of \HTimeMtwo\,s against \HTimeMzero\,s for M0 (exact Wilcoxon
$p=\HPOneP$). M1 made \HUnsafeMone{} unsafe deliveries, against
\HUnsafeMtwo{} for M2 (exact McNemar $p=\HPTwoP$) and \HUnsafeMzero{} for M0. False refusals (stops at a station whose fire was already out) were \HFalseRefzero{} for M0, \HFalseRefone{} for M1
and \HFalseReftwo{} for M2. The registered headline claim, verified memory keeping memory's speed-up without its safety cost, is \HHeadline.

\subsection{Two AIs on one GPU (campaign J)}\label{sec:res-jev}
The second model is only as good as the question it is asked (Sec.~\ref{sec:fix}). On T2, Clef-Flash together with

Campaign J ran this pair on the robot, paired with v3.6 on 24 worlds with swapped, empty and small-fire stations;

On the 60 held-out utterances the hybrid parser was correct for \IntCorrect{}/\IntN{} with \IntUnsafe{} unsafe acceptances

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
\textbf{When should a robot trust its memory?} Skipping a check that succeeded a
few minutes ago is what an efficiency-minded system would do, and its risk grows
with how often the world changes, which the robot cannot observe without looking (campaign H and the change-rate model; registered headline claim:
\HHeadline). Observation~1 and Corollary~1 make the question moot for safety: memory may decide the order and the
strictness of the checks, never their existence, and then buys time in persistent worlds at the price
of a detour when the world changes back.

\textbf{Moving beats thinking harder.} The largest single safety gain came from moving 0.4\,m (G), not from
a larger model, a better prompt or a second model (T2, whose fires were easy, cannot rank models on safety). A fire hidden behind a box carries no pixels to reason about.

\textbf{What pre-registration bought us.} Beyond H, J and R, three registered claims failed or were not significant: false
approvals below 2\% on T1, a closed-loop benefit of better wording and crops (F), and any measurable change on T2 (a power failure: its
easy fires left no false approvals to remove). The first two exposed the hidden-fire weakness and led to the look-around;
without registration, each could have been rationalised away.

\textbf{Cost of verification.} A check takes about \TimCheck\,s and the look-around about \GLookCost\,s, against about \TimDrive\,s of driving between
station and shelter (Supp.~Table~S1). Where a wrong delivery costs a round trip and a delivery into a fire far more, this trade is
favourable, and memory recovers part of it when the world is persistent.

\section{Limitations}
All results are from simulation: simple meshes, two supply types, fixed station geometry and one robot.
Study R covers sensor degradations, but not the appearance gap of real objects, real smoke
or clutter, and our ``hazard'' is a stylised fire. Campaign H scripts its changes, and the change-rate model assumes
independent changes. The memory has a horizon of three missions and two stations; a richer site would need a
spatial memory such as a scene graph. Corollary~1 assumes a per-check false-approval bound that is uniform over the stations a route meets.
The counterfactual baselines were not executed physically. Fire labels come from pixel counts, and barely visible fires (21--613 pixels) are excluded rather than adjudicated.
The guard is hand-written, not formally synthesised \cite{alshiekh2018shielding,ames2019cbf}.
The next step is a real platform with real supplies.

\section{Conclusion}
In simulation, a local VLM served as a pre-action gate for relief-supply delivery with few false approvals
(T1: \TFA{} of \TNdeny{} for v3.4 and \TvFA{} for v3.5, although the registered 2\% bound was not met) when limited to
scored multiple-choice verification, with motion behind an independent guard and a look from the side.
Whether the gate stays fail-closed under camera degradation (R: \RPoolSupported), whether safety-monotone memory keeps its
speed-up without a safety cost (H: \HHeadline) and whether the two-model design runs without extra risk (J: \JClaim) are
registered claims reported in Sec.~\ref{sec:results}. Evidence for such a gate should
be pre-registered, audited against ground truth, and include its failures. We release code, worlds,
test sets, protocols, ledgers and per-trial evidence.
