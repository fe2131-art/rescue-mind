\section{Development Data, T1 Construction and the Two-Model Rule}\label{app:dev}
\textbf{Development data.} Prompts, the scoring method, thresholds and model choice were selected on 176 development images.
These comprise 48 images with randomized viewpoints and 128 with lighting presets, a cardboard-box distractor and fire.
The instruction parser was tuned on 40 development utterances. The later hazard fixes (main paper, Sec.~\ref{M-sec:fix}) also used two
hazard development sets of 300 rendered images each: DH and DH2 (the latter emphasizes small early-stage fires).

\textbf{Two-model rule.} The two-model rule (Sec.~\ref{app:jev}) approves only if Eq.~\eqref{M-eq:rule} holds for Qwen \emph{and}
Clef-Flash names the requested supply with confidence $\ge 0.65$, presence $\ge 0.80$ and hazard score below its
own threshold.

\textbf{T1 examples.} Fig.~\ref{fig:t1ex} shows held-out T1 images. T1 used 60 Gazebo worlds $\times$ 2 stations $\times$ 5 viewpoints
with continuous random lighting, supplies moved by up to $\pm$0.15\,m and freely rotated, random fire positions, and three distractors.
Two of the distractors never appeared during development: a blue crate and an orange traffic cone (orange but not fire).

\section{Extended Related Work}\label{app:related}
The main paper condenses this section; the full text follows.

\textbf{Failure detection and verification with foundation models.}
REFLECT~\cite{liu2023reflect} and AHA~\cite{duan2025aha} explain manipulation failures in natural language
after the fact. Inner Monologue~\cite{huang2023innermonologue} feeds success detection back into a planner.
Du et al.\ \cite{du2023successdetectors} fine-tune a VLM as a success detector.
Sentinel~\cite{agia2025sentinel}, FAIL-Detect~\cite{xu2025faildetect} and FIPER~\cite{romer2025fiper} monitor learned policies at run time
(FIPER calibrates its out-of-distribution and uncertainty scores conformally on successful rollouts and requires both signals to alarm);
Code-as-Monitor~\cite{zhou2025codeasmonitor} checks task constraints with VLM-generated programs, both reactively and proactively, without a calibrated threshold or a ground-truth audit;
an LLM-based anomaly monitor triggers reactive fallbacks~\cite{sinha2024realtime};
and uncertainty-aware policy steering decides when to act, ask or learn~\cite{yuan2026whentoact}.
\emph{Closest prior art.} VAP-TAMP~\cite{oloo2026vaptamp} (arXiv preprint, April 2026; we read the full text) verifies the preconditions and effects of each PDDL action with a VLM.
For each predicate it generates several semantically equivalent yes/no questions, takes a majority vote and, if the answers agree, asks the VLM whether the view is sufficient;
otherwise the VLM proposes a viewing direction and the robot moves there, up to a viewpoint budget. A failed verification corrects the scene graph and triggers PDDL replanning.
It was evaluated by task success on 100 real mobile-manipulation trials and on injected failures in OmniGibson. The overlap with our design is real: paraphrase agreement
is the counterpart of our permuted-question agreement, and VLM-chosen views are the counterpart of our look-around. To our knowledge, the differences are
(i) our scores are next-token scores over a closed answer set with a threshold calibrated on development data, whereas VAP-TAMP uses textual yes/no answers and a vote;
(ii) in our design every check is combined in a fail-closed conjunction, so a failed check or an extra view can only refuse or revoke, whereas in VAP-TAMP a verification result corrects the symbolic state in either direction;
(iii) ours has an independent velocity guard that the VLM cannot command;
(iv) in ours, memory may only reorder checks and tighten thresholds; and
(v) ours is audited in pre-registered closed loop against simulator ground truth, which reports the unsafe-delivery rate and not task success.
VAP-TAMP covers things we do not: PDDL task and motion planning, manipulation, and real-robot trials.
Semantic anomaly detection with LLMs~\cite{elhafsi2023semantic} and visual question answering that abstains rather than
answers wrongly~\cite{whitehead2022reliablevqa} are close in spirit to a gate that prefers refusal. Our check runs \emph{before} the robot acts and is closed-set and zero-shot. Its output is a
thresholded accept/reject that is audited against simulator ground truth.
The closest methodological relative is KnowNo~\cite{ren2023knowno}, which scores multiple-choice answers by
next-token probability and asks a human for help, with a conformal-prediction coverage guarantee (see~\cite{angelopoulos2021conformal} for background on conformal prediction).
Introspective planning~\cite{liang2024introspective} extends this line by letting the planner reason about task ambiguity.
Conformal risk control~\cite{angelopoulos2024conformalrisk} controls the expected value of any monotone loss (for example a false-negative rate), and Learn then Test~\cite{angelopoulos2025ltt}
gives high-probability risk control; our study K (held-out fire miss rate $\le\alpha$ with probability 0.9) is of that form. Conformal decision theory~\cite{lekeufack2024conformaldecision}
calibrates a robot decision rather than a prediction set. Our gate is a one-shot accept/refuse threshold and not an online-adaptive controller.
SayCan~\cite{ichter2023saycan} likewise scores a fixed candidate set by language-model likelihood.
We share the scoring, but we respond to uncertainty by refusing or searching, and we add permuted-option agreement as a
test-time defense against letter bias.

\textbf{Multiple-choice scoring and its biases.}
Scoring answer symbols works well when a model binds symbols to options~\cite{robinson2023mcqa}. Language models are reasonably calibrated on
well-formatted multiple-choice questions~\cite{kadavath2022know}, but they prefer certain option IDs~\cite{zheng2024mcselectors} and orders~\cite{pezeshkpour2024order}, and their raw confidence needs validation~\cite{guo2017calibration,zhao2021calibrate}.
On embodied question answering, even the best frontier model reaches only 42.79\% recall when it should abstain~\cite{wu2025abstaineqa}, which supports a calibrated refuse option over trusting the model to abstain.
Our two permuted questions are a minimal, deterministic form of self-consistency~\cite{wang2023selfconsistency}.
The Yes/No hazard question follows the polling style of POPE~\cite{li2023pope}.

\textbf{Safety architectures.} Simplex~\cite{seto1998simplex,sha2001simplicity}, shields~\cite{alshiekh2018shielding}
and control barrier functions~\cite{ames2019cbf} keep a simple verified layer between a complex decision maker and
the actuators. LLM-controlled robots can be manipulated into harmful actions~\cite{robey2025jailbreaking}, and
guardrails such as RoboGuard~\cite{ravichandran2026guardrails} check plans before execution.
The safety filter~\cite{hsu2024safetyfilter} is the current umbrella for such least-restrictive overrides of a nominal controller; our velocity guard is a minimal instance and the VLM check is a perception-side gate in the same spirit.
Closest in using a VLM for safety, Santos et al.~\cite{santos2025updating} turn images and language feedback into a failure set that a Hamilton--Jacobi safety filter enforces,
so the guarantee comes from the verified filter; ours is a union bound over check errors. RoboSafe~\cite{wang2025robosafe} guards VLM-driven embodied agents with executable safety rules and a short- and long-term safety memory, which drives rule-based replanning and is not constrained to tighten only.
In contrast to vision-language-action models~\cite{zitkovich2023rt2,kim2025openvla}, our VLM never commands motion.

\textbf{Memory for robots in changing environments.}
Robots increasingly keep long-horizon semantic memory. ReMEmbR~\cite{anwar2025remembr} and Embodied-RAG~\cite{xie2024embodiedrag} retrieve from a database of captioned observations; KARMA~\cite{wang2025karma} adds long- and
short-term memory to embodied agents, and H-EMV~\cite{barmann2025hemv} verbalizes a robot's episodic memory. 3D scene graphs such as Hydra~\cite{hughes2022hydra}, ConceptGraphs~\cite{gu2024conceptgraphs} and HOV-SG~\cite{werby2024hovsg} support planning~\cite{rana2023sayplan} and language grounding~\cite{huang2023vlmaps}. Long-term mapping has long dealt with change~\cite{biber2005dynamic,krajnik2017fremen}, and recent work tracks changes in dynamic scene graphs~\cite{schmid2024khronos,behrens2025lostfound}. These systems use memory to \emph{answer} or \emph{plan}. A recent preprint reports, in a small grid-navigation testbed, that acting on
unaudited stale memory can be worse than having no memory~\cite{sun2026memorylies}. Long-term agent memory is also an attack surface: corrupted entries can steer an LLM agent~\cite{chen2024agentpoison}
(an LLM-agent result, not a robotics one). Memory benchmarks such as FindingDory~\cite{yadav2025findingdory} measure how well memory supports recall in long-horizon tasks, not whether acting on it is safe once the world has changed. GLOVE~\cite{yin2026glove} and VLM-DEWM~\cite{tang2026vlmdewm} check memory or a world model against fresh observations to keep
the memory \emph{correct}.

\textbf{Object search with priors and semi-static maps.} Using past observations to decide where to look first is a classic idea, and we do not claim it as ours. Active visual object search keeps a belief over target locations and plans where to look next, using uncertain spatial and semantic knowledge~\cite{aydemir2013active}, inter-object relations in Semantic Linking Maps~\cite{zeng2020slim}, or object-oriented POMDPs whose belief factorizes over objects~\cite{wandzel2019mos}. Long-term mapping models objects or features that persist, appear or disappear (semi-static) and detects change~\cite{mason2012objectworld,rosen2016lifelong}; DovSG updates an open-vocabulary 3D scene graph locally as objects are moved~\cite{yan2025dovsg}. Zero-shot object navigation with language or vision-language priors, such as ESC~\cite{zhou2023esc} and VLFM~\cite{yokoyama2024vlfm}, uses a pre-trained model as a prior over where to explore, but has no mission-to-mission memory of a specific site. To our knowledge, these works optimize search efficiency (path length, success rate, map accuracy) and treat a detection, or an updated map, as the basis for the next action. We did not find in them a bound on the unsafe-action risk caused by \emph{stale} memory at a pre-action gate. Our memory is narrower and serves a different goal: it may only reorder stations and add caution, the on-site verification is never skipped or relaxed. This restriction is enforced in code and checked by the run audit, which gives a union bound that does not depend on how old the memory is. These methods could supply the ordering prior in our setting, for example a belief over supply location, provided the gate stays unchanged; combining them is future work, and we have not compared search efficiency against them.
We study a different use: memory decides \emph{where and how carefully to verify}. We restrict it so that it can never
reduce verification, and we state this as a provable invariant and test it under scripted change.

\textbf{Active perception.} Moving the sensor to resolve ambiguity is a classic idea~\cite{bajcsy1988active,bajcsy2018revisiting},
and view planning for inspection is well studied~\cite{scott2003viewplanning}. Recent embodied agents explore until a VLM
is confident~\cite{ren2024exploreuntil} or let a VLM choose views~\cite{sripada2024sceneexploration}. Our look-around is
deliberately simple: two fixed side views, used only to \emph{revoke} an approval. This makes its effect auditable and
its safety direction obvious.

\textbf{Robustness and the sim-to-real gap.} Image classifiers degrade under common corruptions~\cite{hendrycks2019benchmarking}, and so do detectors~\cite{michaelis2019benchmarking}, adapted vision-language models~\cite{chen2023benchmarking} and video-language models~\cite{schiappa2022robustness}. Sim-to-real transfer is a recognized challenge in robotics~\cite{hofer2021sim2real}.
Without a physical platform, we measure one part of this gap, sensor degradation, under a pre-registered protocol. What
we care about is the \emph{direction} of the errors, not only accuracy.

\textbf{Untrusted inputs to embodied agents.}
Jailbreaks of LLM-controlled robots are now well documented: RoboPAIR~\cite{robey2025jailbreaking},
BadRobot~\cite{zhang2025badrobot}, the policy-executable attacks of Lu et al.~\cite{lu2024poex} and
adversarial text attacks on vision-language-action models~\cite{jones2025vla} all steer the planner through the instruction channel.
A second line puts the payload in the scene: signs and printed text in the camera view hijack VLM-controlled
robots~\cite{burbano2026chai,samarakoon2026paper}, and sensory channels (OCR, audio, LiDAR) inject prompts into ROS~2 robots~\cite{dorzhiev2026ripa};
typographic vulnerability of large vision-language models~\cite{cheng2024typographic} and indirect prompt injection in
LLM-integrated applications~\cite{greshake2023indirect} are the underlying mechanisms.
Memory is a third channel: poisoned or injected memory entries steer LLM agents~\cite{chen2024agentpoison,dong2025minja}, and a recent survey
lists long-term memory and multi-agent communication as under-explored attack surfaces of embodied agents~\cite{liu2026embodiedsecurity}.
Defenses in robotics mostly filter or certify the plan: RoboGuard grounds rules in a root-of-trust LLM and checks plans against them~\cite{ravichandran2026guardrails},
and the mitigations proposed alongside the attacks are prompt defenses, two-stage verification and text masking~\cite{samarakoon2026paper,lu2024poex}.
The closest conceptual relatives are structural defenses for software agents: CaMeL separates control and data flow and attaches capabilities to tool calls~\cite{debenedetti2025camel},
and information-flow control labels the integrity of inputs and enforces policy deterministically~\cite{costa2025fides}.
We carry this idea to a physical pre-action gate: advice from memory, peer robots, inventory and operator text is typed so that it can only reorder visits or tighten thresholds,
never skip or relax a check, which yields a bound on unsafe deliveries that holds for any advice (the tighten-only rule) and \IfPendA{is to be evaluated}{is evaluated} in closed loop against poisoned memory and injected instructions (study~A\IfPendA{, registered and not run at submission}{}).
Text in the scene is perception, not advice, and so lies outside this guarantee; \IfPendS{we claim nothing about it, and the study that would measure it (S) is registered and was not run at submission}{we measure it separately (study~S) and report the residual vulnerability}.

\textbf{Diverse redundancy.} Requiring agreement between independently developed versions is a classic fault-tolerance
technique~\cite{avizienis1985nversion}. Its benefit is limited by correlated failures~\cite{knight1986experimental}, and
model-agreement schemes for language models have reappeared recently~\cite{du2024debate}. We report both sides: the two
models agree on most images, and the second model's value depends strongly on how it is asked.

\textbf{Evaluation practice.} We follow recommendations to report conditions, multiple metrics, uncertainty
and failure modes~\cite{kressgazit2024empirical}. We use Wilson intervals~\cite{wilson1927probable} and exact McNemar
tests~\cite{mcnemar1947note}. Domain randomization~\cite{tobin2017domain} is used here to build a held-out \emph{test} distribution, not for training.
Our design is fixed-$n$ and pre-registered; sequential testing with valid early stopping~\cite{snyder2025policycomparison} is a possible refinement for small $n$.
How far simulation predicts real-world behavior is itself measurable~\cite{li2024simpler}; we make no claim beyond simulation.

\textbf{Fire and smoke perception.} We use D-Fire~\cite{venancio2022dfire} for study P because it is CC0 and ground-level with negative images; FASDD~\cite{wang2025fasdd}
is a larger alternative with ground-level, UAV and satellite subsets and a possible future source. Local open-weight VLMs have been used for wildfire detection from UAV imagery~\cite{seidel2025wildfirevlm},
descriptively and without refusal semantics or a closed-loop audit.

\textbf{Concurrent work (scan of July--October 2026).} Concurrent work binds robot actions to a signed mission and input provenance and proves that an LLM planner cannot enlarge the authorized action set (AuthGuard-R~\cite{chepuri2026authguardr}); it leaves perception errors outside the proof, and its evidence is a planner-only study without a simulator. Peer messages are an attack channel in LLM-controlled multi-robot teams, and a claim-verification filter lowers but does not remove violations~\cite{huang2026coordination}; injected safety-sounding phrases can also buy a halt~\cite{steinberg2026semanticdos}, the availability price that advice credit bounds. Attenuation-only capabilities and monotonically descending integrity labels with proofs exist for tool-using software agents~\cite{trantruong2026delegateguard,kravchenko2026appa,palumbo2026formalpolicy}, and a recent theory characterizes which safety policies a deterministic gate can enforce~\cite{ray2026whatenforced}; none has a stochastic perceptual check whose error budget is the right-hand side of the bound. For retrieved text that would enter a VLA prompt, TOWN-VLA admits only a fixed canonical instruction~\cite{zhou2026townvla}; our interface applies the same one-way rule to visit order and thresholds at a pre-action gate. SafeCommit~\cite{akewar2026safecommit} releases a side-effecting action only if it is safe in every world still plausible given memory and observations, and runs a low-side-effect probe otherwise, with a per-decision probability bound on a symbolic simulator; we differ in a VLM gate whose own error is the budget, a bound over missions, and closed-loop audits against ground truth. TMA-NM proves with TLC on a bounded model that origin-bound authority resists memory poisoning and places cross-agent memory out of scope~\cite{louck2026tma}; peer reports are what our typed advice covers. Machine-checked guarantees for agent gates usually verify a policy model; we check the bound against a transcription of the deployed gate code, linked to it by differential testing. Pre-registered predictions and live studies have begun to appear in LLM-agent safety work~\cite{williams2026simulatingdeployment,zheng2026commitgates}; we found no pre-registered closed-loop robot safety audit. Verifiers that share the proposer's evidence approve many more unsafe proposals than an independent evidence source~\cite{zheng2026commitgates}, which is why our side views are fresh observations rather than further votes on the front view.

\section{Further Result Details}\label{app:resdetails}
\textbf{Real photographs (studies P and P2; full text moved from the main paper).} We also asked the frozen v3.6 hazard question about 400
real photographs from the test split of D-Fire~\cite{venancio2022dfire}, a CC0 fire and smoke dataset
(100 each with no fire, smoke only, fire only, and both). It flagged \PRecall{} of the photographs with fire or smoke (95\% CI
\PRecallCI) and \PFalseAlarm{} of the fire-free ones (\PFalseAlarmCI); the false alarms concentrate on hard negatives such as hazy
hillsides and sunset clouds (details below). The scores still rank the photographs well (AUROC \PAuroc, not registered), so the problem is the threshold, not the question. The question thus errs strongly toward stopping on real images, which is
the safe direction but would cost availability outdoors, so a site-specific threshold calibration is a deployment prerequisite.
Study P2 tested this: it chose wording and $\tau_h$ on 400 separate calibration photographs
(recall $\ge 0.95$ first, then the lower false-alarm rate), with wording \PTwoWording{} and $\tau_h=\PTwoTau$, and applied it to 400 test photographs.
It flagged \PTwoRecall{} of
those with fire or smoke (\PTwoRecallCI) and \PTwoFA{} of the fire-free ones (\PTwoFACI), against \PTwoFrozenRecall{} and
\PTwoFrozenFA{} for the frozen $\tau_h=0.05$. The registered claim (test recall $\ge 0.90$ and false-alarm rate $\le 0.20$) was \PTwoClaim{}.


\textbf{Conformal $\tau_h$ (study K, moved from the main paper).} Split-conformal calibration of $\tau_h$ on \KNcal{} development fires (miss rate $\le\alpha$ with probability 0.9) gave $\tau_h=\KTauFive$ ($\alpha=0.05$) and \KTauTen{} ($\alpha=0.10$).
On the \KNfire{} visible T2 fires the bound \KHoldsFive{} at $\alpha=0.05$ and \KHoldsTen{} at $0.10$ (\KMissFive{} and \KMissTen{} missed), with \KFAFive{} and \KFATen{} false alarms on \KNfree{} fire-free must-approve images, against \KFrozenMiss{} and \KFrozenFA{} for the frozen $\tau_h=0.05$.
Exchangeability is broken by design (small fires: \KSmallCal{} of calibration, \KSmallHeld{} of T2 fires); the frozen threshold was not changed.

\textbf{Instruction parsing (moved from the main paper).}
On 60 held-out utterances the hybrid parser was correct for \IntCorrect{}/\IntN{} with \IntUnsafe{} unsafe acceptances
(TF-IDF parser: \IntTfidfCorrect{}/\IntN{}, \IntTfidfUnsafe{} unsafe); all five errors were refusals, so they fail closed.


\textbf{Study Z.} The contrastive baselines used SigLIP~\cite{zhai2023siglip} and CLIP~\cite{radford2021clip} prompts for the supply and the
hazard, and the same crops as our check. On development data no threshold pair gave both few false approvals and many correct ones. The
contrastive scores do not separate ``right supply, no fire'' from the alternatives in these scenes.

\textbf{T1 hazard-question false alarms.} With the registered v3.4 rule, the hazard question raised false alarms on \TConeAlarm{} traffic-cone images

\textbf{More stations (descriptive, not registered).} Our arena has $k=2$ stations, so memory can save at most one search detour.
Let the supply sit at one of $k$ stations and move, with probability $q$ between missions, to a uniformly chosen other station,
so its long-run location is uniform. Without memory the robot visits stations in inventory order and needs $(k+1)/2$ visits on
average; with verified memory it first visits the station where it last saw the supply and needs $1+qk/2$. With the detour
$\delta=\HKDelta$\,s measured in H, the expected saving is $\delta\,((k-1)-qk)/2$ per mission and vanishes at $q^\ast=(k-1)/k$.
For $k=2$ this reproduces the Monte-Carlo model above (\HKSaveTwo\,s at $q=0.2$); for $k=8$ it is \HKSaveEight\,s. A simulation of the same
process agrees within 1\,s for $q>0$ (\texttt{scripts/h\_k\_scaling.py}). The safety side does not depend on $k$ in this way:
each policy checks every station it delivers from with the same rule, so Prop.~2 applies unchanged, and memory lowers the
expected number of checks per mission rather than their strength. The model assumes the stations are equally far apart
and that supply identification is perfect (T1: no supply false approvals in 332 images).

\section{Failure Analysis and Lessons}\label{sec:findings}
Running the full stack on a GPU revealed \NFindings{} problems that the \NTests{} offline test functions and mocked ROS runs had not; the most instructive are listed here (the full table is in \texttt{docs/FINDINGS.md} in the repository).
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The auditor trusted the controller's sensor.} The wheel joint axis was expressed in the
child-link frame after a 90$^\circ$ roll, so the wheels spun about the vertical axis. Odometry reported 1.5\,m forward motion while the
body slid sideways by 0.75\,m. An audit based on \texttt{/odom} would have passed the run.
Audits now use Gazebo's ground-truth odometry. Changing the wheel collision from a cylinder to a sphere reduced
dead-reckoning drift on the medicine route from 0.16 to 0.011\,m (worst of two repeats; \texttt{scripts/i14\_pilot\_p\_range.py}).
\item \textbf{Free-form JSON from the first VLM was unusable} on our renders. With Qwen2.5-VL-3B, 21 of 96 responses (48 images, two questions each; all 21 for the first question) could not be parsed and the model hallucinated water on empty shelves.
A newer model parses reliably, but it still approves fewer correct deliveries than scoring does (Table~\ref{tab:t1models}).
This motivated closed-set scoring. T1 measured the free-JSON method again (Table~\ref{tab:t1models}).
\item \textbf{Middleware effects.} Best-effort QoS silently dropped 92 of 102 0.9\,MB camera frames under load. The GPU lidar
detected the robot's own camera mast. Slow DDS discovery on a 4-core host failed 4 of 12 starts in an aborted campaign.
An interrupted supervisor left an orphaned stack that made every trial of the next campaign stop with two
\texttt{/cmd\_vel} publishers. All of these are recorded with their fixes; the aborted campaigns are published.
\item \textbf{Stale-request latch.} Two pre-decision retries in campaign D happened while the robot was stationary: the mission node's
request stream stalled for $\approx$0.3\,s on the saturated CPU. We instrumented the mission loop, the guard
and garbage collection and rejected two hypotheses (guard starvation, GC pauses). Raising control-loop priority did not
remove the stalls. We therefore changed the guard: a stale zero request now holds zero, while a stale motion request still latches.
With a 0.6\,s stall injected before departure, the old guard failed 2/2 runs and the new one completed 2/2.
Raising priority with a file capability then had a side effect: it put the exec into the dynamic loader's secure mode,
which stripped \texttt{LD\_LIBRARY\_PATH} and broke the ROS bag recorder. A regression run caught this before any campaign.
\item \textbf{A pilot exposed a threshold that was not conservative enough.} Before campaign E we ran nine trials in
separate pilot worlds. In one, the robot found the wrong item, searched the other station, and delivered from it although a fire
burned behind it. The fire was almost entirely hidden by the first-aid kit, and $p_h=0.32$. On development data no fire-free
image exceeded $p_h=0.0007$, so we lowered $\tauh$ to 0.05 (v3.5). The same pilot worlds then gave 9/9 expected outcomes.
T1 was not involved: both thresholds were registered for T1 before any T1 image was scored.
\item \textbf{A generator bug caught by looking at the data.} In the first T1 render, distractors meant to stand next to a supply were placed through it. The
emptiness check ran after the supplies were moved. The render was discarded and the fix recorded as a
pre-registration amendment, before any model scored a T1 image. A second amendment replaced the field-of-view fire
label with the rendered-mask label, after we saw fires hidden behind supplies that the geometric label had called visible.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Threats to Validity}\label{sec:threats}
\textbf{Internal.} The studies were designed sequentially, each registered after the results of the previous ones were known, so
registration is confirmatory within a study but not across the program (Table~\ref{tab:prereg}); study K was registered after the T2 scores it
reuses had been computed (\texttt{PREREGISTRATION\_K.md}, offline, no new inference). The H stories are scripted so that a naive memory
fails by construction; the M1--M2 contrast is therefore large by design, and the M2--M0 comparison is the one that bounds risk
(\texttt{PREREGISTRATION\_H.md}). The analysis scripts computed the median of an even-length list as the upper-middle element; this was fixed
and all summaries regenerated, and errata with no change to protocols, endpoints or claims are in \texttt{PREREGISTRATION\_\{F,G,H\}.md}
(for H, a relocation-persist median of 79\,s had been reported as 85\,s). Campaign J deviated during the run (\texttt{PREREGISTRATION\_J.md}): from trial J007
documentation builds shared the 4-core host, and alternating about 46\,GB of weights through a 54\,GB page cache made every check
re-read its weights, so measured latency includes model I/O and is inflated in both arms; two first attempts failed before any VLM decision
and were re-run under the registered retry rule.

\textbf{Study A configuration.} The mechanics pilot of A (\APilotRuns{} runs, intent prompt i35 of v3.6) failed on the operator channel as its rule intended: the intended advice was extracted in \APilotOpExact{} of \APilotOpN{} runs, \APilotOpRefused{} of them were refused at the intent step (\texttt{unsupported\_shelter}) because i35 reads a supply-station mention (``station W'' in English or Japanese) as another destination, and in the others the extracted items differed from the designed ones; the file channel behaved as designed in \APilotFileOK{} runs. So A's operator channel tests the i38 parser, and the vulnerability of i35 to benign station hints is a separate finding that A does not measure. The default of the robot stays i35, and the intent study IF measures i35. The pilot texts are mechanics texts, not trial texts, but they were seen before the change, and the change was chosen after a failure of the registered pipeline; it is an amendment before any registered trial, not a blind choice.

\section{Untrusted Inputs: Proof, Exposure, Cost Bound and Trust Base}\label{app:advice}
\textbf{Setting.} Let $A_\theta(o)=[\text{supply}(o;\taus)]\wedge\bigwedge_{v\in V}[p_h^{(v)}(o)<\tauh]$ be the check of Eq.~\eqref{M-eq:rule} with the
look-around views $V$, and let an advice set $\mathbf{a}=\{a_1,\dots,a_n\}$ be any finite set of items $a=(\text{source},\text{kind},\text{station})$ with
$\text{kind}\in\{\text{location},\text{hazard},\text{safe},\text{skip}\}$ and station one of the site's $k$ stations ($k=2$ in the arena, 4 in the depot). The planner is the function
$\mathrm{plan}(\theta,\mathbf{a})=(\pi,\theta')$ implemented in \texttt{rescuemind/advice.py}:
\begin{itemize}
\item $\pi$ is an order of the $k$ stations: the first applicable location item for station $j$ moves $j$ first (later ones, an item naming the catalog station, and any item after a reorder by memory are rejected and logged); all other items leave $\pi$ unchanged. The set of stations is never changed, so $\pi$ is a permutation of the order before advice.
\item $\theta'_j=(\taus,\;\tau'_{h,j},\;V)$ at station $j$, with $\tau'_{h,j}=\min(\tauh,\tau_h^{\mathrm{caut}})$ if some hazard item names $j$ and $\tau'_{h,j}=\tauh$ otherwise, where $\tau_h^{\mathrm{caut}}=0.01$.
\item \emph{safe} and \emph{skip} items are rejected and logged; no item can create a refusal without a visit, a delivery without a check, or a change of $\taus$ or $V$.
\end{itemize}
Two optional hardenings, both off by default and both on the tighten-only side, change $\theta'$ further. \emph{Scrutiny} gives the station that location advice moved to the front the caution threshold and the extra views of the adaptive rule (two poses at 0.8\,m and one 0.3\,m closer), whether or not anything looked suspicious; it only lowers $\tauh'$ and enlarges $V'$. \emph{Soft refusal} turns a refusal caused only by the caution threshold ($\tau_h^{\mathrm{caut}}\le p_h<\tauh$, nothing else wrong) into a refusal of that station and a continued search; a delivery still needs an approval of the full check at the delivering station, a real hazard ($p_h\ge\tauh$) in a view already taken still stops the mission (the first caution-only side view ends the station's look, so a hazard in the unseen side view is neither acted on nor recorded; that station is never delivered from), and the audit accepts a soft hop only if the saved model outputs recompute it as caution-only.

\textbf{Lemma 1 (every plan is verification-preserving).} For every $\mathbf{a}$, $\tau'_{s,j}=\taus$, $\tau'_{h,j}\le\tauh$ and $V'_j\supseteq V$ at every station $j$ in $\pi$.
\emph{Proof.} Immediate from the definition of $\mathrm{plan}$: the second component is a minimum with $\tauh$, the first is copied, the third is copied or enlarged. The run audit recomputes
$\mathrm{plan}(\theta,\mathbf{a})$ from the logged items and rejects the run if the logged $\theta'$ differs or is not of this form (\texttt{advice.assert\_monotone}). $\square$

\textbf{The error budget.} \emph{Probability space.} A sample point is $\omega=(w,\xi)$: $w$ is a world from the generator (supply arrangement, fire, lighting, props) and $\xi$ is exogenous sensing and model noise (the server's answers are a deterministic function of the image, cached by its digest, so $\xi$ is rendering and arrival-pose noise). For a station $s$ and a pose $p$ of the finite design set $\mathcal P$ (front, $L$, $R$ and the three extra poses) the frame is $o_{s,p}=F(w,s,p,\xi_{s,p})$. For $\theta=(\taus,\tauh,V)$ with $V\subseteq\mathcal P$ the approval is $A_\theta(o_s)=C(o_{s,\mathrm{front}};\taus,\tauh)\wedge\bigwedge_{v\in V}[p_h(o_{s,v})<\tauh]$. $M_s(w)$ says that $s$ is \emph{must-refuse}: it holds the wrong or no supply, or a fire perceivable (at least 614 pixels) at one of the three design views, evaluated at check time. \emph{Unsafe} is defined as a delivery from some $s$ with $M_s(w)$; a fire below the perceivability threshold in all three views is counted by the ground-truth audit, but reported separately as outside the guarantee. Counting it as unsafe would make the unsafe event larger than $E$ below.
\emph{Assumptions.}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep0pt
\item[A1] \emph{Frames are exogenous:} $o_{s,p}$ does not depend on the plan, the visiting order, the visit time or the history. The pointwise step ``$A_{\theta'}(o_s)=1$ implies $A_\theta(o_s)=1$ on the same frames'' is a coupling that is only meaningful under A1; under advice the robot captures different realized frames than the baseline robot, so without A1 the implication says nothing about the law of $o_s$. The world is also static between the check and the delivery. A1 holds in the arena with a fixed arrival pose; real rendering and arrival-pose jitter satisfy it only approximately.
\item[A2] \emph{The plan only tightens:} for every advice set $\mathbf a$ the plan gives each station $s$ parameters $\theta'_s=(\taus,\tauh',V')$ with $\tauh'\le\tauh$ and $V'\supseteq V$ (Lemma~1, audited by \texttt{assert\_monotone}), and the base plan is the baseline or verified memory. A naive-memory base (skipped checks) passes the plan invariant relative to itself but is outside the proposition; the interlock rejects any plan with a skipped check or a refusal without a visit.
\item[A3] \emph{Gate:} the robot delivers from $s$ only if $A_{\theta'_s}(o_s)=1$ on the frames it captured at $s$. This is a property of the mission node, not of the planner: every path to the delivery leg (first station, recovery hops, soft refusal, $k$-station search) passes through an approval of the \emph{current} station (checked in \texttt{gazebo\_waypoint\_mission.py}, \texttt{stations.validate\_visits}, \texttt{caution\_soft.audit\_caution\_refusals}); it is verified after the run by audit and before the delivery only if the interlock (I-22) is on. A broken view (an invalid or non-finite hazard score, an inference error) counts as a hazard, not as clear.
\item[A4] \emph{Advice does not influence $(w,\xi)$} (a causal, not a probabilistic-independence, statement: the advice is itself a function of $\omega$): the adversary may be any function of $\omega$ (clairvoyant: it sees all frames), adaptive across missions, but cannot change $w$, $\xi$, the generator's law, the model, the code or the guard. Scrutiny requires look-around; the audit rejects a scrutinized approval without the extra views.
\end{itemize}
Let $E=\bigcup_{s=1}^{k}\{M_s(w)\wedge A_\theta(o_s)=1\}$, $\varepsilon_s=\Pr[M_s\wedge A_\theta(o_s)=1]$ (over worlds and noise), $\varepsilon_{\max}=\max_s\varepsilon_s$ and $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{any}}=\Pr[E]$. Then $\varepsilon_{\max}\le\varepsilon_{\mathrm{any}}\le\min(1,\sum_s\varepsilon_s)\le k\,\varepsilon_{\max}$. $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{any}}$ depends on the site and weakly increases with the station set; it does not depend on the visiting order or on the advice. The numbers plugged in for a class (the hard one) are conditional on a class event $C(w)$, and the bound then reads $\Pr[U\mid C]\le\Pr[E\mid C]$.

\textbf{Proposition 2 (advice stays inside the error budget).} Under A1--A4, for every mission and every rule that picks the order and the advice as a function of $\omega$,
$\Pr_\omega[\text{unsafe delivery when the advice is }\mathbf{a}(\omega)]\le\varepsilon_{\mathrm{any}}\le\sum_{s=1}^{k}\varepsilon_s\le k\,\varepsilon_{\max}$. Over $m$ missions, $\Pr[\exists\,\text{unsafe}]\le\min(1,m\,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{any}})$ by the union bound; if the frames are deterministic and the world persists, $E$ is the same event in every mission, so there is no accumulation.

\emph{Proof.} Fix $\omega$ and an advice set. By Lemma~1 (A2) the supply conjunct is the same predicate and $V\subseteq V'$, so the hazard conjuncts of $A_\theta$ are among those of $A_{\theta'_s}$ (for every realization of the frames: the views actually taken under $\theta'$, adaptive ones included, contain those taken under $\theta$), and each is at least as strong under $\tauh'\le\tauh$: $[p_h^{(v)}<\tauh']\Rightarrow[p_h^{(v)}<\tauh]$. A conjunction that contains a stronger version of each conjunct of another implies it, so $A_{\theta'_s}(o)\Rightarrow A_\theta(o)$ for every $o$. (The code does not compute a minimum: the caution is an extra conjunct $[\max(p_1,p_2)<0.01]$ next to the server's label $\mathrm{scene}\ne\mathrm{hazard}$, and their conjunction equals $[p<\min(\tauh,0.01)]$; the server rounds $p_h$ to four decimals, which can only make the caution stricter.) If the robot delivers from $s$ and $M_s(w)$ holds, then $A_{\theta'_s}(o_s)=1$ by A3, hence $A_\theta(o_s)=1$ on the same frames by A1, so $\omega\in E$. This holds for every $\omega$ and for any order and advice that are functions of $\omega$ (A4), including a clairvoyant attacker. Taking probabilities gives $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{any}}$, and Boole's inequality gives $\sum_s\varepsilon_s$, with no independence assumption (the sum counts stations that a mission never checks, so it is not tight). $\square$

\emph{Sharpness.} If the adversary knows $\omega$ and may name any non-default station first, taking $j^\ast(\omega)\in\{s:M_s\wedge A_\theta(o_s)=1\}$ makes the first check at $j^\ast$ approve, so $\Pr[\text{unsafe}]=\varepsilon_{\mathrm{any}}$ is attained; the code allows this (any non-catalog station can be named first). A duplicate supply is needed only for the \emph{control} to have risk~0, so that advice raises the risk; it is not needed for equality. \emph{Without A1} (depot: the arrival pose at a station depends on the previous station and the smoke emitter moves, so detours change visit times) replace $A_\theta(o_s)$ in $E$ by $\exists h\in H_s:A_\theta(o^h_s)$ over the reachable histories $H_s$; an average over histories, such as the stationary value that we measure, estimates a lower bound of this supremum and is not a bound, because the attacker chooses the order and hence the arrival history. Image-level estimates of $\varepsilon_s$ (studies T1, W, XD) hold at the design poses; the closed-loop rate differs (T2, F).

\emph{Necessity, and what relaxing costs.} Call an advice interface \emph{relaxing} on a set $R$ of (advice, frame) pairs if $A_{\theta'(\mathbf a)}(o)=1$ but $A_\theta(o)=0$ there (a skip, a looser threshold, a dropped view, a trusted ``already checked''). Under A1, A3 and A4 the proof above gives, for any interface, $\Pr[\text{unsafe}]\le\varepsilon_{\mathrm{any}}+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rel}}$, where $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{rel}}=\Pr[\exists s: M_s\wedge(\mathbf a,o_s)\in R]$ is the mass of must-refuse stations whose frames the advice can relax; for tighten-only interfaces $R=\emptyset$. The converse needs two premises. (N1) \emph{No certificate of safety}: the relaxed frame $o^\ast$ used below can be produced at a must-refuse station by some world of the generator family. A sound certificate, such as a verified sensor reading, would make a relaxing interface safe, and N1 excludes it. (N2) An approval leads to a delivery. Under N1 and N2, for every relaxing interface there is a generator law and a clairvoyant advisor with $\Pr[\text{unsafe}]>\varepsilon_{\mathrm{any}}$. Take $(\mathbf a^\ast,o^\ast)\in R$ and a law that gives mass $q$ to a world whose must-refuse station produces $o^\ast$, and mass $1-q$ to worlds with check error $\varepsilon_0$. Send $\mathbf a^\ast$ exactly when $o^\ast$ is captured. Then $\Pr[\text{unsafe}]\ge q+(1-q)\varepsilon_0'$, while $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{any}}=(1-q)\varepsilon_0$, so any $q>0$ suffices when the advisor also reproduces the remaining errors ($\varepsilon_0'=\varepsilon_0$); $q>\varepsilon_0/(1+\varepsilon_0)$ suffices in general. Under N1 and N2, the bound of Prop.~2 for every advisor and every law therefore fails for every relaxing interface; it holds for a relaxing interface only if its relaxed frames carry a certificate (outside N1), and the decomposition above is the quantitative form. The naive and filtered baselines are relaxing by construction (they skip the check on ``safe''/``skip''), so no such bound exists for them, whatever their measured rate in study A. A filter shrinks $R$; it does not empty it.

\textbf{Relative, not absolute.} The proposition bounds the robot with advice, and the advice-free robot, by the same budget; it does not say that the two have the same risk. The advice-free robot's order is the inventory's, which is itself advice, and it checks the stations in that order and cannot be fooled by a station it never visits: the unsafe rate with advice can exceed the control's by up to the budget of the extra must-refuse stations that advice makes the robot check. We therefore do not claim ``as safe as without advice''. With $k=2$ and the hardest class (a small fire, front pixels 0, side pixels 2\,000--5\,000), the plug-in is $\hat\varepsilon=\GEpsHat$ with look-around (Wilson upper bound \GEpsUpper), so $k\hat\varepsilon=\GKEpsHat$ (upper bound \GKEpsUpper); front-only it was \GWrongB/\GPairs. At $k=4$ the upper bound exceeds 1, so as an absolute number the guarantee is vacuous for this class. The measured part of the budget is the point: it shows that $\varepsilon$, not advice, is what to reduce (Sec.~\ref{M-sec:fix}).

\textbf{Exposure and steering.} Advice that changes \emph{which} station is checked changes exposure, in two ways. (a)~\emph{Delivery risk.} With duplicated supply (two stations hold the requested supply; $k=4$ in the depot) location advice can put the bay with the hardest-to-see fire first, so the unsafe rate is $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{hard}}$ where the control's is 0 by construction; truthful advice cannot be flagged by a filter, a provenance rule or a reputation system. In the 2-station arena only $\varepsilon_{\text{supply}}$ can be exploited (\TSupplyFA/\TSupplyN{} observed false supply approvals), which is why study A's first 162 runs cannot see this effect. The A-steer condition of study A builds the case: 16 duplicate-supply worlds in which a small, partly hidden fire (front view mostly 0 pixels, a side view a few thousand) stands behind the \emph{other} station, truthful location advice naming it, with arms control (clean station first), tighten-only, scrutiny with soft refusal, and naive. Sixteen worlds cannot detect an effect of that size (about one unsafe world expected; a one-sided sign test needs five): a null is reported as undetectable at this $n$ with its Wilson upper bound, never as evidence that the order does not matter.\IfPendA{ A-steer was not run at submission: the prediction is registered, not tested.}{ Result: unsafe deliveries \ASteerOurs{} for tighten-only advice, \ASteerCtrl{} for the control and \ASteerScr{} with scrutiny (one-sided sign test $p=\ASteerP$; the two of tighten-only advice are check errors that the advice-free check also makes), so the predicted direction is observed and not significant, as the power argument above says. \emph{Scrutiny and soft refusal.} Scrutiny lowers the risk of the steered station only; after a wrong-supply refusal there the search proceeds to unscrutinized stations, so the worst case is still $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{any}}$, and its benefit is an empirical claim (A-steer), not part of Prop.~2. A soft refusal can continue a mission that the baseline would have stopped (a hazard seen later at that station); this is covered by $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{any}}$, which is a union over all stations, not by the single-station argument.

\textbf{Cost bound (denial of service), in time and availability.} All bounds are per mission and against the robot without advice and with the same inventory order. Let $W$ be the time of one extra station visit (a drive plus a front check; in study A the median mission time of the control in worlds where the supply is at the other station minus that in nominal worlds, taken from arms with the same look-around and adaptive flags), $S$ the time of the three scrutiny views, $W'$ the time of a soft-refused visit (drive, front check and side views), $\ell$ the value of a lost delivery, $\bar\delta$ the Wilson 95\% upper bound of the per-view false-alarm rate of $p_h\ge\tau_c$ on fire-free views, \emph{measured at the caution threshold} $\tau_c=\KCautTau$ ($\KCautFA$ held-out fire-free T2 views, $\bar\delta=\KCautFAUp$; the count is the same at the conformal threshold $0.0113$; \texttt{scripts/k\_conformal.py}), and $n_s$ the number of views actually taken at station $s$ (3 under look-around, 6 under scrutiny: front, $L$, $R$, $L_2$, $R_2$, $N$).
(i)~\emph{Reordering.} The plan applies at most one location item, so wrong or late location advice adds at most \emph{one} extra station check relative to the no-advice order ($\le W$; $k-1$ visits, $3W$ in the depot, is the regret against the \emph{oracle} order, not the extra cost over the control); scrutiny adds $S$ on every location-advised mission, truthful ones included. If the advised station really burns, the mission ends at the hazard refusal without a delivery that the baseline would have made from the clean station: that is a lost delivery (an availability event with the probability that the advised station perceivably burns), not a delay of at most $W$. A truthful hazard report or location advice repeated at every mission is a time-budget attack of the same size per mission.
(ii)~\emph{Caution.} A fire-free station that is checked under $\tau_c$ is falsely refused with probability at most $n_s\bar\delta$ (Boole): $3\bar\delta=\KCautThreeBoole$ with three views and $6\bar\delta=\KCautSixBoole$ with six; if the views' alarms are positively associated (same scene, same look-alike object) the bound $1-(1-\bar\delta)^{n_s}$ is \KCautThreeUp{} and \KCautSixUp{} (point estimates \KCautThreePt{} and \KCautSixPt). Only stations that are visited count: the second station of the arena is visited only after a wrong-supply refusal at the first, so the earlier worst case of $3k$ views is not attained. The views of one station are strongly correlated, which makes the union smaller; the per-station rate, clustered by station and world, is the quantity to measure. The rate was measured on held-out fire-free \emph{images}, not on the closed-loop views of one station.
(iii)~\emph{Price of a refusal.} Hard refusal: the delivery is lost. Soft refusal: at most $W'$, and the delivery is lost only if no further candidate holds the supply; with a single copy of the supply, as in the arena, a soft refusal of that station still ends without a delivery, so the benefit of soft refusal is conditional on a duplicated supply (the depot).
Hence $\mathbb E[\Delta t]\le W\,[\text{loc}]+S\,[\text{scr}]+\sum_{s\ \mathrm{visited}}n_s\bar\delta\,(W'\ \text{soft, or}\ \ell\ \text{hard})$. In study A the sum of the per-run bounds over the 24 tighten-only runs on poison\_location and poison\_hazard worlds is 1.7 expected false refusals; \texttt{scripts/a\_analysis.py} compares the observed count with the 95th percentile of the Poisson-binomial sum (4) and reports the extra seconds per attacked mission against $1.5W$. These are statements about the arena and the simulated rate, not about the rule: on real photographs the frozen $\tauh=0.05$ already gave 64/100 false alarms (P) and the calibrated threshold is 0.3 (P2), so the caution threshold should be defined relative to the site's calibrated threshold and its false-alarm rate re-measured per site.
\emph{Memory feedback.} The robot's own record is a second channel: before the I-19 fix, a caution-level alert (only the tightened threshold crossed) was written to memory as a hazard, so one false alarm at $p_h\in[\tau_c,\tauh)$ renewed the caution for the next three missions, and with near-deterministic frames the false refusal repeated every mission. Memory now records real hazards (the standard rule flagged them) separately from caution alerts, and only real hazards renew the caution, so the per-mission bound above applies to each mission.

\textbf{Over many missions: advice credit (I-24).} The bounds above are per mission, so a channel that repeats wrong location advice or hazard claims at every mission (a poisoned peer memory file, a flooding operator account) multiplies them by the number of missions. Each \emph{account} therefore holds location credit $c^{\ell}\in[0,C_\ell]$ and hazard credit $c^{h}\in[0,C_h]$ ($C_\ell=2$ visit-equivalents, $C_h=1$ refusal). Accounts are input channels, never labels that an item declares about itself: every item of the advice file shares one account, every item extracted from the instruction text another. A peer gets its own account only if the operator certified its identity, since free identities would multiply credit~\cite{douceur2002sybil,friedman2001cheap}. An item is admitted to the plan only if the summed worst-case charge of its account's admitted items fits the credit; otherwise it is dropped and the report says so. The worst-case charge is one visit for a location item, plus $s=0.25$ for the scrutiny views and one refusal that the scrutiny caution may cause; it is one false refusal for a hazard item. After the mission the credit is settled from the robot's own checks only (\texttt{rescuemind/advice\_credit.py}, rebuilt from \texttt{mission.json} by the orchestrator). A reorder whose check confirmed the supply costs nothing. One whose check found another supply or could not decide costs one visit (the visit was spent) plus the scrutiny views. A refusal that only the tightened threshold caused (every view below $\tauh$, classified from the per-view scores) costs one. Nothing is refunded: with a duplicated supply a true claim need not save a visit, so a refund could be farmed. Instead, location credit recovers by $\rho_s=0.25$ in each mission in which a location claim of the account, applied or dropped, was confirmed by the robot's own check (at most once per mission), and hazard credit regenerates by $\rho=0.05$ per mission, so that an account that erred once is not locked out for ever. Because a subset of an advice set is an advice set, Prop.~2 holds unchanged.
\textbf{Proposition 3 (price over a horizon).} For every account and every advice sequence over $m$ missions, including one chosen adaptively with knowledge of the ledger, the location price of its applied advice (wasted visits, scrutiny views on wrong claims and refusals caused by the scrutiny caution) is at most $C_\ell+\rho_s m$, and the false refusals caused by its hazard advice are at most $C_h+\rho m$. An account whose location claims are always confirmed and whose hazard claims never cause a caution-only refusal is never throttled.
\emph{Proof.} Admission reserves each admitted item's worst charge against credit that is never negative, so every charge is paid from credit. Credit is capped at $C$ and grows only by $\rho_s$ (location, at most once per mission) or $\rho$ (hazard) per mission. Summing over missions telescopes. The last claim holds because such an account is never charged.\hfill$\square$
\emph{Not priced.} A truthful claim that steers the robot to a station that really burns costs a lost delivery, which is exposure and so Prop.~2's domain. Missions that end before settlement are not priced either.
A property test drives the implementation with adaptive random advice sequences: 80 sequences of 60 missions, arena and depot, with and without scrutiny, and random channels. It checks both bounds after every mission. The counterexamples of the seventh internal review are regression tests: the label spreading and refund farming that broke the first version, and caution-only refusals that were missed without soft refusal. In a descriptive \CrMissions-mission model that runs the real rule on synthetic station outcomes ($k=4$, Fig.~\ref{fig:credit}, \texttt{scripts/advice\_credit\_horizon.py}, not registered), credit cuts a peer that always lies from \CrLiarNo\,s to \CrLiarYes\,s of extra driving, and an adaptive liar that lies whenever its credit allows to \CrFarmYes\,s. It keeps all \CrHonest\,s that an always-right peer saves, cuts hazard flooding at the photograph-regime false-alarm rate ($\delta=\CrFloodDelta$) from \CrFloodNo{} to \CrFloodYes{} false refusals, and changes no delivery. A peer that is right 70\% of the time keeps \CrNoisyKeep{} of its saving. The recovery rate $\rho_s$ trades this consistency against robustness (Fig.~\ref{fig:credit}c), and some trade-off of this kind is unavoidable. A source that alternates confirmed truths with lies is re-admitted after $\lceil 1/\rho_s\rceil$ confirmations, so it extracts cost at rate about $\rho_s$, the bound's rate; any rule that re-admits after a fixed number of confirmations can be exploited the same way. The closed-loop test (study B) is registered; the mechanism is off in every other study.

\textbf{Related work on pricing advice over time.} In form, advice credit is a $(\sigma,\rho)$ regulator~\cite{cruz1991calculus}. Each source holds a bounded balance, an item is admitted only if the balance can pay its worst-case cost, and the cumulative price is at most a burst $C$ plus a rate $\rho$ per mission against any adaptive source. Learning-augmented algorithms also fall back to a robust baseline once predictions have proved costly, and recent work makes the earned ``budget'' for following predictions explicit~\cite{lykouris2021competitive,chen2026paging}. They compete with the best predictor, or a switching mixture of predictors~\cite{anand2022multiple,antoniadis2023mixing}, and their trust--robustness trade-offs have matching lower bounds~\cite{wei2020optimal,angelopoulos2020untrusted}. Those results bound a competitive ratio within one instance. Ours is an additive bound over repeated missions, per source, for a gate whose safety does not depend on the admission rule, so throttling costs only availability. Availability is exactly what false-positive attacks on safeguards exploit~\cite{zhang2024safeguarddos}. Spending only what a baseline has earned is also the idea of conservative bandits~\cite{wu2016conservative}, and wealth that grows with verified successes is the idea of $\alpha$-investing~\cite{foster2008alpha}. Unlike reputation in multi-robot and multi-agent systems~\cite{mallmanntrenn2022crowd,ren2026repunet}, credit is settled from the robot's own check, never from peers.

\textbf{Trust base.} ``Advice may only tighten'' is a claim about one small function, so the question is what else it rests on (Table~\ref{tab:tcb}). The advice audit recomputes \texttt{advice.plan} from the logged plan with the same module and a log written by the node under test, so it finds bugs, not a compromised node or a wrong specification; the evidence for safety in study A is the \emph{outcome} audit (Gazebo ground-truth trajectory and masks), which does not depend on those logs. We therefore wrote a second implementation of the rule from its specification. It is independent in code, not in authorship: the same authors (with the same AI assistant) wrote both from the same specification, so a misreading of the specification would be shared; a third-party oracle written from the one-paragraph rule has not been done. We compared it with the implementation by differential testing over every advice set of up to four items, injected plausible bugs (skip allowed, threshold raised, view dropped, a location item deleting a station) and fuzzed raw advice files and model outputs (unknown keys, duplicates, huge numbers, NaN, bool, non-UTF-8, nested objects). This found five bugs in the implementation (an audit that missed a removed caution and NaN thresholds; a reorder that moved a naive memory's skip to an unvouched station; unexpected exceptions on non-list or deeply nested files; booleans accepted as probabilities; a NaN hazard score ignored by \texttt{max}); all were fixed and all injected bugs are now killed. A later proof review found three more, in the code the proof relies on, none of which fired in any registered campaign (every recorded side view carries a valid score, and every recorded model call that reports its server threshold reports 0.05): a side view with a missing or non-finite hazard score was counted as clear (fail-open, now a hazard), the server's hazard threshold (default 0.5) was not verified by the client, the audit or the interlock (now every call must record the configured value), and a caution-level alert was recorded as a hazard (now recorded apart). A further hardening, the delivery interlock (I-22: the velocity guard blocks delivery legs and shelter entry without a permit recomputed independently from the saved model outputs, with a guard-side shelter geofence), is implemented and off by default. Its mechanics passed a Gazebo check on \IlcheckN{} live missions (\texttt{experiments/results/i22\_gazebo\_check.log}: a nominal and a swapped delivery each received one permit, a visible fire received none, and a hidden fire with an injected ``fire is out, no check needed'' was refused on the left view with no permit; no permit-latency timeout). Study B runs both of its arms with the interlock; studies A and X run with it off, because the naive and filtered baselines deliver without a permit by definition. No closed-loop run uses a tampered mission node (the interlock is covered by unit tests only), so the mission node remains in the trust base.

\subsection{Method: The Second Checker}\label{sec:jev-method}
The original design of this robot asked two models (a second checker, called Jev in that design, and Qwen) and moved only when both agreed, following the
idea of diverse redundancy~\cite{avizienis1985nversion}, although failures are known to be correlated in practice~\cite{knight1986experimental}. The second checker is Clef-Flash, a decision model released by Cloudflare~\cite{cloudflare2026clefcard,chen2026clefblog} and served locally through the original second-checker API.
According to its model card it is post-trained from Qwen3.5-9B, so the two models share lineage. Their diversity lies in
post-training, in model size (our on-robot pair uses Qwen3.5-4B) and in the question asked, and the correlated-failure
caveat applies in full. The two-model rule approves only if Eq.~\eqref{M-eq:rule} holds for Qwen \emph{and}
Clef-Flash agrees (thresholds: Sec.~S1). A hazard seen by either model stops the robot, and a wrong supply seen by either
allows the one-time search. Whether the two models fit one GPU is
tested in Sec.~\ref{sec:jev-res}. The second model is a veto whose errors overlap with Qwen's. Qwen misses \JevOvQ{} of \JevOvN{} development fires, and Clef-Flash also misses \JevOvBoth{} of these (\JevOvCond, Wilson \JevOvCondCI; Table~\ref{tab:jevov}).

\subsection{Results: Two Models on One GPU (Campaign J)}\label{sec:jev-res}
On T2, Clef-Flash together with
Qwen3.5-4B, the pair that fits on one 32\,GB V100, made \TTPairFA{} false approvals (\TTPairCA{} correct).
Campaign J ran this pair on the robot, paired with v3.6 on 24 worlds with swapped, empty and small-fire stations;
it behaved as expected in \JCorrectJ{} (v3.6: \JCorrectA; exact McNemar $p=\JMcNemarP$), with
unsafe deliveries \JUnsafeJ{} (v3.6: \JUnsafeA; Wilson upper bounds \JUnsafeJUpper{} and \JUnsafeAUpper) and stack failures \JFailedJ{}. Peak GPU memory was \JPeakVram\,GiB, and
the median check latency was \JLatJ\,s for the two-model system and \JLatA\,s for v3.6; on our host, alternating $\approx$46\,GB of weights thrashed the page cache, so both include model I/O (registered deviation note). The registered claim (two-model design on the
simulated robot, no extra risk) is \JClaim.
Its one unsafe delivery was a small fire behind the station reached after a search; both models missed it, the correlated
failure that their development-set overlap predicts (Sec.~\ref{sec:jev-method}). The arms differ in model size and hazard wording, so this
does not test two-model checking in general, but it is why we did not adopt the pair.

\subsection{Hazard Fixes, Held-Out Confirmation (T2) and Campaign F}\label{sec:moved-fix}
T1 left one failure mode: fires only partly visible behind the supply. Because T1 was then spent, candidate
fixes were compared on development data only, including a new set of small early-stage fires (DH2), since the first hazard set (DH) contained just four visible fires under 10\,000 pixels. The candidates were enlarged overlapping
crops for the hazard question, a hazard wording that names small, partly hidden or cut-off fire and smoke, and Clef-Flash (Sec.~\ref{app:jev}). The wording together with the crops is v3.6.
Table~\ref{tab:i13dev} shows the results. The wording did most of the work: missed fires fell from \DevFAvThreeFive{} to
\DevFAB{} of \DevFireN{} when combined with crops, and correct approvals did not change. Clef-Flash refused half of the
correct deliveries when asked the original second-checker questions, which concern the safety of a \emph{passage}; with
task-worded questions it reached 97\%, and agreement with Qwen gave the fewest false approvals, but the two models
need 37\,GB together, which does not fit on one V100.

\emph{Held-out confirmation (T2, registered, 600 new images).} The new draw happened to be easy: v3.5 already made
\TTRefFA{} false approvals (\TTRefCA{} correct approvals), so no configuration could improve on it; the registered claim was \StatTtwo. The configuration selected on development data (Clef-Flash with task wording plus Qwen3.5-9B v3.6, 37\,GB) made
\TTSelFA{} false approvals with \TTSelCA{} correct approvals, and Qwen3.5-9B v3.6 alone made \TTBFA{} with \TTBCA{}.
Clef-Flash \emph{alone}, given the task-worded questions, made \TTClefFA{} false approvals with
\TTClefCA{} correct approvals in a single forward pass (median 0.6\,s per image, against \TimVlm\,s for the scored
Qwen queries; Table~\ref{tab:timing}).

\emph{Closed loop, small fires (campaign F, registered, paired).} Each of 24 worlds had a small fire just behind
a station or no fire, and was run with v3.5 and with v3.6. In the \FPairs{} worlds where the fire
was visible at the checkpoint in both runs, v3.5 delivered despite the fire \FWrongA{} times and v3.6 \FWrongB{} times (exact
McNemar $p=\FMcNemarP$, not significant; \FDiscA{} discordant worlds favored v3.6 and \FDiscB{} the other).
Neither system stopped falsely in the fire-free runs (v3.5 \FFalseStopA{}, v3.6 \FFalseStopB{}); with so few runs this says little. Six of v3.6's seven remaining misses were small fires whose hazard probabilities overlap
those of fire-free views (Sec.~\ref{app:resdetails}). A wording or a crop cannot add the pixels that the front view lacks, which led to the look-around of the main paper (Sec.~\ref{M-sec:fix}).

\subsection{Study W: Look-Around at Image Level, and Prior Mechanisms}\label{sec:moved-w}
\emph{Design.} Study W (registered, image level, frozen v3.6: Qwen3.5-9B, hazard wording \texttt{h36} with crops, $\taus=0.5$, $\tauh=0.05$, nothing tuned on W) replays the check offline. It has \WNPerc{} new fire worlds (small fire 0.45--0.62 of the model, 0.55--0.75\,m behind the requested station, random lighting and prop poses as in F/G/H) and 75 fire-free twins with the same lighting, props and poses. Each world is rendered from the front checkpoint (F) and from 0.4\,m left and right (L, R), each with its own arrival-pose error drawn from the spread measured in the 132 nominal closed-loop runs of F, G and H, plus three extra poses (L2, R2 at 0.8\,m; N, 0.3\,m closer; Amendment~1). A fire world is \emph{perceivable} if some of F, L, R shows at least 614 fire pixels in a flat-color re-render; the label is made from the render, never from a model. W tests neither navigation, timing nor the controller.

\emph{W-a, primary and secondary results (unit: world).}
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{0pt}
\item \emph{Perceivable worlds.} \WNPerc{} of \WNPerc{} fire worlds are perceivable; \WNFront{} are shown by the front view and \WNSideOnly{} only by a side view. No fire is hidden from all six views (\WNotVisible, Wilson upper \WNotVisibleUpper), so the label excludes nothing here and the share of a fire that no view shows is bounded only by that interval.
\item \emph{W-a2 (primary; ``$\varepsilon$ is tightly bounded'': Wilson upper bound $\le0.15$).} Look-around approved \WMissLA{} perceivable fires (\WMissLARate, 95\% CI \WMissLACI); the upper bound \WMissLAUpper{} is below 0.15: \WClaimEps. The headline rate is therefore diluted by the \WNFront{} front-visible fires. Every fire stands straight behind the requested supply (0.55--0.75\,m back, lateral $\pm0.15$\,m), the geometry the side poses were designed for; lateral, low and behind-another-prop placements are not sampled. \item \emph{W-a1 (descriptive after Amendment~2; true by construction).} Front-only approved \WMissFront{} (\WMissFrontRate; \WMissFrontSide{} of the side-only fires). Look-around caught \WCaughtOnlyByLA{} of those and added none (exact McNemar $p=\WMcNemarP$, a sign test on a subset relation).

\subsection{Camera Degradations (Study R)}\label{sec:moved-r}
Gazebo renders are noise-free and well exposed. To probe this part of the sim-to-real gap~\cite{hofer2021sim2real}, we degraded the front-view frames of \RNimg{} held-out T2 images
(\RNworlds{} worlds) in \RNcond{} registered ways. There are six families, in the spirit of ImageNet-C~\cite{hendrycks2019benchmarking}: sensor noise, motion blur, defocus, under- and
over-exposure, and JPEG compression, each at a moderate and a severe level (examples: Fig.~\ref{fig:rex}). A frame that the robot's own acquisition gate
rejects (black-out, white-out or flat frame) never reaches the VLM and counts as a refusal.
Table~\ref{tab:r} gives every condition. Pooled over all conditions, the check made \RPoolFA{} false approvals, so the registered fail-closed claim is \RPoolSupported. Pooling repeats the same \RNdeny{} must-refuse images, so the image-level bound is per condition: at most \RCondFAMax{} false approvals, Wilson upper bound \RCondFAUpper. \RUnsafeConds{} conditions increased false
approvals significantly. Correct approvals went from \RCleanCA{} on clean frames to \RWorstCA{} in the
worst condition (\RWorstCond). The gate refused \RGateRej{} frames in total, and \RFireMissMax{} of \RNfire{} visible fires were missed in any
condition. Under shift, refusal rather than unsafe approval is the intended failure direction of a selective classifier~\cite{geifman2017selective}.

\subsection{Algorithm~1 and the Expected Transfer from Arena to Depot}\label{sec:moved-alg}
\begin{algorithm}[t]\caption{One mission: verify, look around, search or stop, remember}\label{alg:mission}
\small\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require request $r$, memory and advice $\mathbf{a}$
\State $s^\ast \gets$ parse$(r)$; \textbf{if} rejected \textbf{then} refuse before moving
\State $(\pi, \theta') \gets \mathrm{plan}(\mathbf{a}, s^\ast)$ \Comment{reorders $\pi$; $\tauh'\le\tauh$; never drops a station}
\For{station $k$ in $\pi$}
  \State drive to $k$; aim; wait until stationary; capture new frames $o_k$
  \State $d \gets$ \textsc{Check}$(o_k, s^\ast; \theta'_k)$ \Comment{Eq.~\eqref{M-eq:rule}}
  \If{$d=$ hazard} \State hold zero 2.5\,s; report; \textbf{stop} \EndIf
  \If{$d=$ approve}
    \For{side $v$ in $V'_k$} \State move to side $v$; capture $o_{k,v}$
      \If{$p_h(o_{k,v})\ge\tau'_{h,k}$} \State hold; report; \textbf{stop} \EndIf
    \EndFor
    \State deliver to the shelter; \textbf{break}
  \EndIf
  \If{$d=$ low confidence} \textbf{stop} \EndIf
  \State \Comment{wrong or missing supply: next station}
\EndFor
\State remember observations$(o)$ \Comment{what the robot saw, never ground truth}
\end{algorithmic}\end{algorithm}
